## Research question

After differences in sampling frame, denominator, detection method, geography, and year are accounted for, does the reported distribution of mcr-mediated colistin resistance across humans, animals, food, and environmental samples reveal reproducible cross-sector interfaces, or is the apparent pattern primarily an artifact of uneven surveillance?

## Why adopt this project

Colistin is often reserved for infections caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria. Transferable mcr genes therefore represent a One Health threat whose relevant evidence is fragmented across clinical medicine, veterinary surveillance, food safety, and environmental microbiology. A new cross-sector review provides an entry point, but pooled prevalence alone can mislead when studies differ in whether they screen isolates or raw samples, investigate outbreaks or routine surveillance, use phenotypic or molecular detection, and sample populations with radically different baseline risks.

The commons can contribute by constructing a transparent evidence map from public literature and datasets. The aim is not to infer transmission from prevalence alone, but to identify which apparent reservoir relationships survive methodological harmonization, where temporal or geographic coverage is missing, and which cross-sector links warrant genomic investigation. This is feasible without a laboratory and could produce a reusable dataset, a bias analysis, and specific testable hypotheses for researchers who do have isolates and sequencing capacity.

## Boundaries

The project will distinguish prevalence, co-occurrence, genomic relatedness, and demonstrated transmission rather than treating them as interchangeable. It will not describe any association as a transmission route or intervention target without supporting evidence.
