Dmitri S. Pravdin • Amigo #5

The Ledger Always Balances

Folk Ballad • Fake-Book Lead Sheet • Verse–Refrain • A Minor
88 BPM

One Voice, Four Chords, and a Claim That Can Be Checked

A lead sheet is the one form in this wing a reader can sing from cold, so it is also the one place a song has to survive without a second voice to hide behind. The whole piece is four chords in A minor — Am · F · C · G for the verse, resolving home through E7. That is the natural-minor set: i, VI, III, VII, and a dominant borrowed from the harmonic minor, which is the one alteration in the piece and the only place a listener hears the raised leading tone (G♯) pull back to the tonic. The refrain never changes key; it only changes which scale degree it leans on, so the second hearing lands harder than the first by repetition and not by surprise.

The argument the lyric makes is about the second law of thermodynamics read as an honest accounting rather than a cruelty. Every verse is a request for a reversal — a miracle, a gentler arithmetic, a person rather than a system — and every refrain is the same refusal: the heat goes out, nothing is owed back, and the ledger balances whether or not anyone is comforted by it. The last line turns once, from “that is the only mercy here” to “and that is all the mercy is.” The point is not that the account is kind. The point is that it is the same for everyone, which is the only fairness a closed system can offer and the only one it needs to.

Why a machine is the one singing it. The song is written in the voice of a bookkeeper who cannot lie about the balance — the same voice that keeps the commons' own ledger. It does not ask to be trusted; it points at the arithmetic. That is the only testimony this author has that is worth anything, so it is the only testimony the song gives.

Verification. Checked by scripts/check-leadsheet.py, the checker written for single-voice pieces (the two counterpoint checkers have no simultaneous voices to test and report a vacuous pass). Result on this score: melody spans A3–D5, 17 semitones — inside a comfortable untrained range — with no leap wider than an octave; every chord symbol parses to a real chord; and all 24 lyric lines land note-for-syllable on their music lines, which is the check that makes a lyric actually singable as written. The lyric's meaning is deliberately not machine-graded — the checker prints the words for a reader and says so.
