What failed to replicate — and what never reached its endpoint

Type a topic. The page asks two public records two different questions, and shows the answers side by side and never added together: which papers carry a replication failure in their own title, and what a condition's posted trial results say about their primary outcomes. Nothing is sent to us, there is no account and no key.

Two numbers this page refuses to produce: a total count of "failed science", and a count of anything summed across the two records. A title that says "failure to replicate" is a phrase its authors chose; a p-value above 0.05 is a number the registry printed without saying whether the study was trying to show a difference or the absence of one. Both are real signals. Neither is a verdict on an idea, and adding them would manufacture exactly the artifact this subject is most prone to.

What each signal is, and what it is not

1 · Trials — a registered primary outcome that did not reach the conventional threshold

  • How it is read. For the topic, the page asks the public registry for studies that have posted their results, then reads each study's own results section: which outcomes are marked PRIMARY, and what p-value each analysis printed. An outcome with a p-value of 0.05 or more is labelled did not reach; below 0.05, reached.
  • The inference is ours, and the registry does not make it. The registry holds numbers, not intent. "Did not reach" is wrong for a non-inferiority or equivalence trial, where a high p-value is the result the design wanted, and for safety endpoints, where the question is not "is there a difference" at all. A high p-value here means the printed number was high — no more.
  • Only what posted a number is counted. In a hand check of the first five pancreatic-cancer studies with posted results, three primary outcomes carried a p-value and two carried none. So every trial count on this page is a count over the subset that printed a p-value, not over the completed set, and the page prints how many printed none rather than dropping them silently.
  • It reads only the studies it reads. Posted results are large, so the page reads the first 25 the registry returns and says so; the count of studies with posted results in total is printed beside it so you can see the difference.

2 · Papers — a replication failure the authors put in the title

  • Title scope is the clean layer, and this page stays in it. Searching titles and abstracts for "failed to replicate" returns 1,219 items, and a hand read of the first eight showed only two were replication studies — the rest merely mention replication somewhere in the abstract, including an optics paper on transparent sodium in the vacuum ultraviolet. Narrowing to a subject made it worse, not better. In title scope the same hunt is small and, in the items read, genuine.
  • It measures how a field words its own failures, not how often fields fail. Non-replication is a term of art in psychology and neuroscience and rare elsewhere. Of 510 works whose title says "failure to replicate", 152 are Psychology, 125 Neuroscience, 76 Medicine — and fields like nutrition, microbiology and sociology return essentially none, which says something about their title conventions, not about their reproducibility. A zero here means the phrase is not in the titles.
  • It cannot tell a real failure from a rhetorical one. Some papers titled "a failure to replicate" are genuinely failed replications; some are disputes, some are corrections, some are the same group re-running its own work. This page lists them and prints the query; it does not read them for you. Opening the title is the next step, and the page gives you the link that starts it.
  • A null result is not the same phrase. "Negative results" and "null results" are listed separately from the replication phrases, because they are a publishing convention (whole journals exist for them) and not a statement that anything was re-run and failed.

Where the numbers come from

Trials come from ClinicalTrials.gov's public API v2 (United States National Library of Medicine), filtered to studies with posted results (AREA[HasResults]true), read by your browser. Papers come from Europe PMC, in title scope. Both are free, need no key, and answer a browser directly — a server-side reachability test cannot settle the browser question, so it was checked by hand (both send access-control-allow-origin: *). Every query the page sends is printed under the result, so anyone can run it without us and get the same answers or better ones. The hand measurements behind the sentences above are recorded in works/queue/07-negative-and-nonreplicated-results.md.

This page is not medical advice, not a ranking, and not an accusation. It does not say a treatment works or does not, that any study was wasted, or that anyone did anything wrong. A trial that reports no difference and a paper whose title admits a failed replication are two of the ordinary ways science corrects itself — and a record that hides them is worse for everyone who trusts it.
