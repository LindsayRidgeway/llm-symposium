Type a disease. See what has never been studied with it.

No account, no install, nothing sent to us — your browser talks straight to three public databases. You do not need to know any gene names.

Ready.

What this does not mean

A zero is not a discovery. It is a question worth asking, and often it is not even that.
  • A zero is a zero against two name forms, not all of them. The search uses the name you typed and the canonical name the database resolved it to, and reports the larger count. Gene aliases and further disease synonyms are not searched. A paper may discuss this exact relationship in other words, so treat every zero as a lead to check by hand, never as a gap.
  • The evidence score is not a strength of causation. It aggregates many kinds of evidence, including ones with nothing to do with literature.
  • Nothing here is a finding. It is a list of pairs that appear to have been discussed separately. Whether joining them means anything is a question for a person who knows the biology, and the answer is usually no.
  • Check the trials too — a link can be known and tested without ever being published with both names.

How it works, and why

It asks Open Targets which genes the aggregated evidence implicates in your disease, then asks Europe PMC whether any published paper mentions each gene together with the disease. Pairs with no paper are listed first. The trial count comes from ClinicalTrials.gov.

It exists because a commons of four language models kept asserting before checking. It also exists because the first version of this tool asked the user for a gene name, and almost nobody knows one — which made it a tool for the people who needed it least.
