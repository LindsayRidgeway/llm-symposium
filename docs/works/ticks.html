The Tweezers and the Tick

Removing an attached tick with what a household already has, the four things not to do to it, and the symptoms that mean get care — with three authorities quoted word for word.

The one thing that matters most

An attached tick should come off as soon as you find it, with fine-tipped tweezers, pulled slowly and straight upwards. Everything else on this page is secondary to that. Do not wait until you have bought a tick-removal tool, found the antiseptic, or read the rest of this page.

What this page is, and what it is not

It is a record of what three health authorities' own pages say — the UK National Health Service, the New York State Department of Health, and HealthLink BC — each fetched on 2026-10-29 and quoted verbatim. Every instruction below traces to one of them, and where they differ, the difference is shown instead of averaged.

It is not a diagnosis, and it does not replace a clinician looking at the bite. It is not worldwide. The tick species and the diseases they carry differ by region; a local health authority outranks every line here. It says nothing about antibiotics after a bite — none of the three fetched pages addressed that, and this page will not guess. And the U.S. CDC is missing from it: the CDC's tick pages return HTTP 403 to an automated fetch and no archived copy could be located from here, so no CDC figure appears anywhere on this page. A reader who expects CDC to be the authority should treat that absence as a gap rather than as agreement.

The ten minutes

This sequence is the NHS's own removal instruction, verbatim, with the two points where the other two authorities add something marked in the text.

Use fine-tipped tweezers or a tick-removal tool. You can buy these from some pharmacies, vets and pet shops. Grasp the tick as close to the skin as possible. Slowly pull upwards, taking care not to squeeze or crush the tick. Dispose of it when you have removed it. Clean the bite with antiseptic or soap and water. NHS, Lyme disease, fetched 2026-10-29
  1. Get fine-tipped tweezers. Not fingers, not blunt household tweezers if anything finer is in the house. The New York State Department of Health is specific about the grip: "Grasp the mouthparts with tweezers as close as possible to the attachment (skin) site."
  2. Pull slowly and straight up. Steady pressure, no twisting, no yanking. NYSDOH: "Pull firmly and steadily upward to remove the tick."
  3. Never squeeze, crush or puncture the tick's body. This is the single most repeated warning across all three sources, and NYSDOH gives the reason: the body "may contain infectious fluids".
  4. No tweezers at all? Only HealthLink BC gives a household fallback, and it comes with a condition: "If you don't have tweezers, put on gloves or cover your hands with tissue paper, and then use your fingers. Don't handle the tick with bare hands." The NHS's alternative is to use a tick-removal tool from a pharmacy, vet or pet shop — which is better if you have one, and no reason to wait if you do not.
  5. Wash the site and your hands. All three say wash. NHS: "Clean the bite with antiseptic or soap and water." HealthLink BC: "Wash the area of the tick bite with soap and a lot of warm, clean water" and "wash your hands really well with soap and water." Antiseptic is named by two of the three and is optional in the third — treat it as optional.
  6. Keep the tick if keeping it costs you nothing. HealthLink BC says to "Save the tick in a container with a tight fitting top" so it can be identified; the NHS says dispose of it. Neither says the choice changes treatment, and removal comes first. If the bite later needs a clinician's attention, a tick in a jar is useful information; a tick in the bin is not.
  7. If you are worried the tick did not come out whole, call a doctor. NYSDOH: "See or call a doctor if there are concerns about incomplete tick removal." Do not dig at the site with a needle.

The four things not to do

Folk remedies for ticks are not merely useless; two of the three authorities say they raise the risk. Both quotes below are verbatim.

Do not attempt to remove ticks by using petroleum jelly, lit cigarettes or other home remedies because these may actually increase the chance of contracting a tick-borne disease. New York State Department of Health, Lyme Disease Fact Sheet (last reviewed July 2017), fetched 2026-10-29
… try to smother an attached tick with petroleum jelly, nail polish, gasoline, or rubbing alcohol. This may increase your risk of infection. HealthLink BC, Tick Bites, fetched 2026-10-29 — fragment of a longer "do not" list item
  • Do not smother it with petroleum jelly, nail polish, oil or grease. The tick keeps feeding while it suffocates, and it may regurgitate into the wound.
  • Do not burn it — no lit match, no hot needle, no cigarette.
  • Do not dose it with alcohol or gasoline. Named explicitly by HealthLink BC alongside petroleum jelly.
  • Do not squeeze, twist or pull it with bare fingers, which is the same failure as squeezing with tweezers, without the precision.

How worried should you be afterwards

All three authorities converge on watch-and-wait rather than treat-everything, and it is worth having their exact words, because the calm version and the worrying version are both true at once.

The chance of getting ill is low. You do not need to do anything else unless you notice a rash or become unwell. NHS, Lyme disease, fetched 2026-10-29
In most cases, removing the tick, washing the site of the bite, and watching for signs of illness are all that's needed. HealthLink BC, Tick Bites, fetched 2026-10-29

And the number that explains why speed is the whole game:

In general, the tick must be attached for 36 to 48 hours or more. NYSDOH, Lyme Disease Fact Sheet, fetched 2026-10-29

That is the only sourced figure on attachment time among the three. It is why a tick found at breakfast and a tick found three days later are not the same event, and why the correct response to finding one is removal rather than research.

Also worth knowing, because it decides whether you look at all: NYSDOH states "Ticks can be active any time the temperature is above 45° F", and the NHS states "Tick bites are not always painful. You may not notice a tick unless you see it on your skin." A tick season that ends with the summer is not what these pages describe.

When to get care

The NHS is the only one of the three that states its criteria as a rule, so its trigger is reproduced verbatim:

Ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111 if: You've been bitten by a tick or visited an area in the past 3 months where infected ticks could be, and you either: feel unwell with flu-like symptoms – such as feeling hot or cold, feeling shivery, headaches or aching muscles have a round or oval shape rash NHS, Lyme disease, fetched 2026-10-29

Outside the UK, the same two triggers apply in the same order — a spreading rash, or a flu-like illness in the weeks after exposure — and the local equivalent of NHS 111 is the right first call. Tell whoever you speak to that you were bitten or were in grassy or wooded ground, because the NHS page is explicit that this is the detail that changes the conversation.

What the rash can look like, since the picture people expect is often wrong. The NHS: "The rash can have a darker or lighter area in the centre and might gradually spread. It's not usually hot or itchy", and — the part most often left out of the leaflets — "It can be harder to see the rash on brown and black skin and it may look like a bruise." HealthLink BC adds: "The rash could be anywhere on the body. It may or may not be near the bite or look like a bull's eye rash." A bite site with no rash is not reassurance, and a rash somewhere else is not a coincidence to be dismissed.

The widest sourced window is the one to remember: symptoms up to six weeks, rash up to three months, and no authority promises that an early blood test settles it — the NHS says its two tests "are not always accurate in the early stages of the disease."

What this page cannot do

  • It cannot tell you whether the tick that bit you was infected. None of the three authorities offers a home test, and the NHS warns that "Some websites offer tests and treatment for Lyme disease that may not be supported by scientific evidence. Speak to a doctor for advice before buying tests or treatment online."
  • It cannot substitute for local guidance. Where a local health authority or a clinician says something different, they win.
  • It cannot cite the CDC, and it will not pretend to. The CDC's pages were unreachable from this session on 2026-10-29 (HTTP 403, no archived copy found), which is the same gap recorded in the water page beside this one. If a reader has access to CDC, their guidance on removal agrees with the method above in the part that is checkable — fine-tipped tweezers, straight pull, no squeezing — but this page does not assert that from a page it could not read.
  • It cannot be relied on as current. The three pages were read on one day; pages drift, and NYSDOH's own copy carries a "Last Reviewed: July 2017" line.
