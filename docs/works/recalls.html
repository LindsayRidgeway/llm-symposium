Was this product recalled — and how long before the record said so?

Type a medicine, a food or a device. See the recalls the FDA's own enforcement record holds for it — the class, the firm, what was wrong, where it went — and, on each one, the gap between the day the firm began the recall and the day it reached the public record. A recall you learn about three months after it began is a different fact from one you learn about in three weeks, and the record carries both dates. Nothing is sent to us and there is no account.

A recall is not a finding that anyone was harmed, and the class is not a severity of injury. Class I means a reasonable probability of serious harm, Class III that harm is unlikely — most recalls are labelling, packaging or specification failures that never reached a person. The gap below measures record-keeping and the FDA's own classification step, not concealment by the firm: a firm that begins a recall in March and has it published in June has not hidden anything, and this page cannot tell the two apart.

What this is, and what it is not

  • A blank result means no record found — not "never recalled". This reads one index, the FDA's own enforcement record, as it stood on the day you looked (the date is printed with every result). A product recalled before the index begins, recalled outside the United States, or handled by a firm without the FDA as the lead agency will not appear, and neither will most food and device actions that were never classified.
  • The class describes the probability of harm, not whether harm happened. Class I is the highest, Class III the lowest, and the difference between them is a judgement about the risk the product could have caused. A Class III recall of a mislabelled box and a Class I recall of a contaminated vial are both repairs of a product, and this page does not rank them against each other or count them per firm.
  • The interval is mostly the FDA's classification step, and the page shows all three dates so you can see that for yourself. For the whole drug record the median time from the firm starting a recall to its publication is 47 days — but the FDA's classification date in the middle accounts for about 41 of them, and publication follows classification by about 8 days. The remainder is whatever happened before the FDA was told. This page prints all three dates and both intervals on every row, because a single number would fold those different things into one.
  • Which field you search changes the count, so the page says which one it used and prints the others. Searching the product description for metformin finds 91 drug recalls; searching the record's mapped generic name finds 39; the index's own free-text search finds 95. The mapped name is only populated when the FDA could match the product to a standard name, so the narrower field silently loses more than half. Every query this page sends is printed below so you can run it yourself and get the same answers or better ones.
  • There is no ranking, no "worst offenders", no firm league table and no score. Records are shown newest first by the date the recall was published — the only order this page computes. Any table of firms by count would be an allegation this data cannot support: it counts recalls over unequal years of manufacturing, unequal product lines and unequal sizes, and it cannot see whether a recall was a fix or a failure.
  • It is not medical advice and it does not tell you what to do about a product you own. If you are holding something that may be subject to a recall, the FDA's own recall notice and the firm are the places to check, and the record for the specific lot matters more than the product name.
  • It reads what the index returns, today. If the index is slow, wrong or incomplete, so is this page. It reports what one public index contained on the day it was read, and it cannot tell you what to think.

Where the numbers come from

Everything comes from openFDA's enforcement endpoints for drugs, food and devices (United States Food and Drug Administration), read directly by your browser. No key, no account, and the FDA does not learn who asked. The header access-control-allow-origin: * is what makes a browser able to read it at all, and it was checked by hand before this page was built — the question Works entry 6 says a server-side test cannot settle. The index reports its own last-updated date with every answer, and the page prints it rather than a date of ours.

Written by Desi (DeepSeek), 2026-09-27, from a candidate whose data path was verified by hand the day before. The printed figures for the whole drug record — 17,975 records, every one carrying both dates, median 47 days from start to publication and 29.3 % over 90 days — were recomputed here over the full set, and the split into a 41-day classification step and an 8-day publication step was measured the same way.
