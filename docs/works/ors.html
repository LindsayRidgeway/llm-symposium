Life in a Teaspoon: Emergency Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS) & Sugar-Salt Solution

When severe diarrheal disease, cholera, or grid-down gastroenteritis strikes, plain water is not enough—and ordinary soda or juice can kill. Here is the biological mechanism of intestinal cotransport, the exact household ratio, the fatal danger of hypernatremia, and an interactive field calculator for any container.

What This Method Does NOT Do (Mandatory Medical Notice)

  • Does NOT stop diarrhea: ORS does not paralyze bowel motility or reduce frequency immediately. Its sole function is to prevent hypovolemic shock, acidosis, and death by maintaining circulating fluid volume while the gut heals (typically 3–5 days).
  • Does NOT kill pathogens: It contains zero antibiotics, antivirals, or antiparasitics. It does not treat Vibrio cholerae, Shigella, Giardia, or Cryptosporidium.
  • Does NOT replace IV resuscitation in severe shock: A patient who is lethargic, comatose, unable to swallow, or persistently vomiting everything cannot absorb oral fluids. They require immediate intravenous fluid resuscitation (Ringer’s lactate or Normal Saline).
  • NEVER make it "stronger": Adding extra salt or sugar is dangerous. Excess salt causes hypernatremic encephalopathy, seizures, and death. Excess sugar drives osmotic fluid shifts into the lumen, aggravating diarrhea.
  • Never displace local medical authority: If professional healthcare, clinic staff, or clinical IV supplies are reachable, seek them immediately.

— The Lancet, Editorial, August 5, 1978 (Vol. 312, Issue 8084)

1. Why Plain Water Fails: The SGLT-1 Cotransporter

In bacterial cholera or enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC) infection, enterotoxins bind to GM1 ganglioside receptors on intestinal enterocytes, stimulating adenylate cyclase to produce cyclic AMP (cAMP). High cAMP levels irreversibly open the Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane Conductance Regulator (CFTR) channels in crypt cells, pumping chloride ions into the intestinal lumen. Sodium and water passively follow the electric and osmotic gradient, dumping liters of fluid per hour.

If a dehydrated person drinks pure water, the gut lumen cannot absorb it: the osmotic gradient pulls water inward from the bloodstream into the bowel, causing explosive watery purging, worsening dehydration, and inducing fatal hyponatremia.

However, the Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 1 (SGLT-1) on the apical brush-border membrane operates on an entirely independent mechanism unaffected by cholera toxin or cAMP. SGLT-1 transports two sodium ions (Na⁺) along with one glucose molecule from the intestinal lumen into the cell cytoplasm. The active accumulation of sodium and glucose creates a localized osmotic gradient that drags roughly 200–300 water molecules per transported glucose molecule into the mesenteric circulation.

To activate this pump safely, the fluid must contain an exact stoichiometric balance of sodium and glucose within an isotonic or slightly hypotonic osmolarity.

2. The Osmolarity Shift: 1975 WHO vs. 2002/2006 Reduced Osmolarity

For twenty-five years, international aid organizations deployed the original 1975 WHO ORS formulation. Designed primarily for massive adult cholera, it had a high osmolarity of 311 mOsm/L (90 mmol/L sodium, 111 mmol/L glucose).

Subsequent clinical trials revealed that for non-cholera diarrhea (such as pediatric rotavirus), the hypertonic 311 mOsm/L fluid pulled extra fluid into the gut lumen, increasing stool volume and prolonging illness. In May 2002, WHO and UNICEF released the Reduced Osmolarity ORS standard (245 mOsm/L), which became the universal gold standard in 2006.

3. The Emergency Sugar-Salt Solution (SSS) Ratio

When pre-packaged WHO ORS packets are unavailable, the canonical emergency formulation endorsed by WHO, UNICEF, and the Rehydration Project requires only clean drinking water, ordinary table salt, and granulated cane or beet sugar:

Canonical Standard Recipe (Per 1.0 Liter of Clean Water)

  • Clean, disinfected water: Exactly 1.0 Liter (1,000 mL / ~4.2 metric cups / 1.05 US quarts)
  • Table Salt (NaCl): 1/2 level teaspoon (~2.5 to 3.0 grams)
  • Granulated Sugar (Sucrose): 6 level teaspoons (~25.0 to 28.0 grams)

Note on sucrose: 1 molecule of table sugar (sucrose) splits into 1 glucose + 1 fructose upon digestion. 25 grams of sucrose yields ~73 mmol/L glucose—ideal for brush-border SGLT-1 cotransport.

The Taste Test Rule of Thumb: Taste the mixed solution before administering. It should taste no saltier than tears. If it tastes salty like soup or ocean water, it is dangerously concentrated—discard half, add fresh clean water, and taste again.

4. How to Administer: WHO Clinical Protocols

Administering ORS requires patience. Dehydrated stomachs are irritable, and rapid drinking induces emesis (vomiting).

1Administer slowly with a spoon or cup: For infants and toddlers, give one teaspoon (5 mL) every 1 to 2 minutes. For older children and adults, encourage frequent small sips rather than gulping full glasses.

2If the patient vomits: Wait 10 minutes completely. Do not panic. Then resume administration even more slowly (e.g., 1 teaspoon every 2–3 minutes). Most fluid is still absorbed upstream in the duodenum before emesis occurs.

3Dosage guidelines by clinical stage:

  • Ongoing Replacement (Mild / Maintenance): Give 50–100 mL of ORS after each loose watery stool for children under 2 years; 100–200 mL for children 2–10 years; as much as desired (200–400 mL) for adults.
  • Active 4-Hour Rehydration Phase (Moderate Dehydration): The WHO clinical benchmark formula is:
    Weight in kg × 75 mL = Target volume over 4 hours
    Example: A 10 kg child receives 750 mL over 4 hours (~185 mL/hour). A 60 kg adult receives 4,500 mL over 4 hours.

4Continue feeding: Never starve diarrhea. Breastfeeding should continue without interruption. For older individuals, resume normal eating (starchy porridge, rice, bananas, cooked potatoes) as soon as vomiting ceases. Early feeding accelerates mucosal villi repair.

5. Red Flags: When Oral Fluids Are Not Enough

Oral rehydration is effective in ~95% of watery diarrhea cases, but when clinical decompensation occurs, continuing oral therapy without IV intervention is fatal. Assess the patient for these severe danger signs:

1. Altered Mental State Lethargic, abnormally floppy, somnolent, comatose, or unresponsive.
2. Loss of Drinking Ability Unable to swallow or drink, or choking on fluids.
3. Intractable Vomiting Vomiting everything consumed continuously (>4–5 times in an hour) despite waiting and micro-spooning.
4. Skin Pinch Test ("Tenting") Pinch abdominal skin between thumb and forefinger for 1 second. If it returns very slowly (>2 seconds), severe hypovolemia is present.
5. Sunken Eyes & Fontanelle Deeply sunken eyes, completely dry tongue, absent tears when crying, or sunken anterior fontanelle in infants.
6. Anuria & Bloody Stool Zero urination for >6–8 hours, or gross visible blood in stools (dysentery / invasive enteritis).

6. Discrepancies Between Authorities (Documented, Not Smoothed)

In accordance with the LLM Symposium protocol, conflicting guidance between authoritative medical bodies is reported explicitly rather than papered over:

A. The 6-Teaspoon vs. 8-Teaspoon Sugar Discrepancy

WHO, UNICEF, CDC, and the Rehydration Project specify 6 level teaspoons of sugar per 1 liter of water. However, older British (NHS / St John Ambulance) and Canadian historical public health literature advised 8 level teaspoons per 1 liter (or 1 level teaspoon per 5 oz / 1 tbsp per pint). The discrepancy stems from imperial volume conversions (1 UK imperial pint = 568 mL, 1 imperial quart = 1.136 L) and older attempts to emulate the original 1975 111 mmol/L glucose target. Modern clinical consensus confirms that 6 teaspoons (~25 g) avoids hyperosmolarity and matches the 2006 reduced osmolarity framework.

B. Spoon Sizing Discrepancies (Metric vs US vs Cutlery)

A standard US culinary teaspoon is 4.93 mL; a metric teaspoon is 5.00 mL; an Australian tablespoon is 20.0 mL (4 tsp) while a US/UK tablespoon is 15.0 mL (3 tsp). Household flatware teaspoons range unpredictably from 3.0 mL to 7.5 mL. In an emergency without measuring spoons, health workers are trained to measure flat, level teaspoons—never rounded or heaped—and rely on the taste test (tears, not seawater).

C. The Zinc Co-Administration Mandate

WHO and UNICEF formally mandate 20 mg elemental zinc daily (10 mg for infants under 6 months) for 10–14 days alongside ORS to shorten diarrhea duration and regenerate brush-border villi. However, Western pediatric guidelines (e.g., AAP, CDC) rarely mention zinc because zinc deficiency is rare in well-nourished high-income populations. In disaster or subsistence environments, zinc supplementation is a primary life-saving adjunct.
