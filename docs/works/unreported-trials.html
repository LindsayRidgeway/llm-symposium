The study finished. Does the record say what it found?

Type an illness and see the studies the public registry lists as completed — not the ones recruiting. For each, two questions are asked of two separate public indexes: does the registry hold a results summary for this study, and does any indexed paper name the trial's registration number. Nothing is sent to us and there is no account.

A missing results summary is not an accusation. Many completed studies are not required to post results, and many report in a journal without ever updating the registry. This page reports what two public indexes contain, not what was learned — and a study can be a success, fully published, and still show blank here.

What this is, and what it is not

  • A blank first verdict means the registry holds no results summary. It does not mean the study had no results, produced nothing, or did anything wrong. Posting results to ClinicalTrials.gov is required under United States law only for some studies, on some timelines, and only since 2017 — and the rule has been revised since, including in 2024. Older studies, studies outside the United States, and studies of unapproved products sit outside it.
  • A blank second verdict means we could not find a paper that names the trial. It does not mean none exists. Journals do not require a registration number in the abstract, some papers name the trial in a supplement or a data-sharing statement rather than in the indexed text, and Europe PMC's index is large but not the whole literature. Treat a blank as not found here, which is all it is.
  • The two columns disagree on purpose, and the disagreement is the useful part. A study with no registry results but a paper that names it is a study that reported somewhere else — which is the common, legitimate case, and you can see one by typing NCT01935063. A study with neither is a question that the public record does not answer on the day you look. It is a lead to a paper trail, not a finding about anyone.
  • There is no ranking and no list of worst offenders. Studies are listed by the calendar — most recently completed first — which is the only order this page computes. No sponsor league table, no count of "missed" studies by company, no score. Any such table would be an allegation this data cannot support, and inventing one is how a page like this becomes a weapon instead of a record.
  • Estimated dates are marked. A completion date the registry marks ESTIMATED is a plan, not a fact; those entries say so, and a study whose date cannot be read is counted separately rather than assumed overdue.
  • It reads what the registry returns, today. If the registry or the index is slow, wrong or incomplete, so is this page. Both queries are printed below so anyone can run them without us and get the same answers or better ones.
  • It does not say whether a treatment works, whether a study was worthwhile, or whether anyone did anything wrong, and it is not medical advice. If you are looking for what happened to a study you or someone you know was in, the study team and the registry record are the places to ask.

Where the numbers come from

Studies come from ClinicalTrials.gov's public API v2 (United States National Library of Medicine), filtered to COMPLETED, read directly by your browser. Every completed study for the condition is read, page by page, so the summary count below is of the whole set the registry returns, not a sample — the page reads up to six pages of 1,000 and says so if it stops early. The paper check goes to Europe PMC by registration number. Neither requires a key and neither learns who you are.
