The Cold Chain: what to keep and what to throw out when the power goes out

A fridge holds its cold for hours, not days. After that, every household has the same two questions — is this still good? and can I refreeze it? — and the strange part is that the four authorities who publish the answer do not agree with each other. This page gives you their own rules side by side, tells you when they disagree, and runs a clock for your outage. It does not tell you whether your particular food is safe. Nothing on a web page can.

The thing this page cannot do

It cannot tell you whether a specific item is safe to eat. The FDA says it in its own words: you cannot rely on appearance or odour, and perishable food that was not kept cold enough "may cause illness if consumed, even when they are thoroughly cooked." A clock measures hours, not bacteria. If you have a thermometer, its reading matters more than anything below; if you do not, treat every number here as a prompt to be careful, not a clearance. When in doubt, throw it out.

The three clocks, as their authors wrote them

Ask a single question — how long may perishable food sit at or above 40 °F before it must be thrown out? — and the three reachable authorities answer it three different ways. This is not a summary of them; it is them.

The window between two hours and four hours is where the FDA and FEMA disagree. The calculator above shows both clocks whenever your reading lands in it, and chooses neither: this page's authors are not qualified to overrule either agency, and the honest move is to hand you the disagreement and the thermometer.

Two numbers on one FDA page, and why that is not an error

40 °F and 45 °F appear on the same FDA page, which looks like a contradiction until you read what each one is for.

  • 40 °F is the keep-or-toss and refreeze line: “If the freezer thermometer reads 40° F or below, the food is safe and may be refrozen.”
  • 45 °F is a narrower, eat-it-now line: “Perishable foods with temperatures that are 45°F or below (measured with a food thermometer) should be safe, but should be cooked and consumed as soon as possible.”

So between 40 °F and 45 °F the food is not refreezable and not keepable — it is cook it now. The calculator reflects that, which is why its verdict for a fridge reading of 43 °F is not “throw it out”.

What to do with this, before and during

  • Before: FDA says put a thermometer in the fridge and one in the freezer (freezer at or below 0 °F, fridge at or below 40 °F), freeze water containers for ice, group food together in the freezer, and freeze items you will not need immediately.
  • During: keep the doors shut. That single habit is what the 4-hour and 48-hour figures assume — every one of them is stated “if the door remains closed”. FDA suggests dry or block ice for a long outage.
  • When the power returns: read the thermometer first. FDA: if the freezer reads 40 °F or below the food is safe and may be refrozen; if there is no thermometer, check each package, and ice crystals or 40 °F or below mean safe to refreeze or cook.
  • Any flooding at all: this page stops applying. FDA discards any food that may have touched flood water unless it is in a waterproof container, and discards cardboard juice/milk/formula boxes and home-canned food outright. That is a different rule for a different situation.
