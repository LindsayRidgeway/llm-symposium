Where may I run it? Carbon monoxide after the power goes out

The power goes out, the generator comes out of the garage, and the one machine that restores the electricity is also, in the United States, the consumer product most often linked to a death that nobody sees coming. Carbon monoxide is colourless and odourless, and the authorities who publish the rule agree on the shape of it — never indoors, 20 feet away — while disagreeing on almost every number around it. This page gives you their own words, tells you where they disagree, and runs a placement check. It cannot measure your air. Only a detector can.

The thing this page cannot do

It cannot tell you whether the air in your house is safe right now. It reports what three agencies published on 2026-10-07; it does not measure anything, and it cannot see the wind, the direction your exhaust faces, or the shape of your building. A carbon-monoxide detector is the only instrument in the room, and the absence of an alarm is the absence of a warning. If you have symptoms and any doubt at all, the CPSC's instruction is a single sentence: get outside to fresh air immediately, and then call 911.

The rule, in the three authorities' own words

Ask one question — where may this be run — and the three reachable authorities answer it in sentences that are not the same sentence. This is not a summary of them; it is them.

All three land on 20 feet. They differ on 20 feet from what — the CDC says windows, doors and vents; the CPSC says homes, with the exhaust facing away; FEMA says windows, doors and attached garages. The widest of the three is the one this page treats as safe, and it says so rather than averaging them.

Two more rules of the same shape, in the same words: the CDC says Never heat your house with a gas oven. and FEMA says Do not use a gas stove or oven to heat your home. It increases your risk of carbon monoxide poisoning.

The numbers they do not agree on

1 — How many people it kills

The two figures look like a contradiction and are not:

  • CDC: “Each year, more than 400 Americans die from unintentional CO poisoning not linked to fires, more than 100,000 visit an emergency department, and more than 14,000 are hospitalized.”
  • CPSC: “More than 200 people in the United States die every year from accidental non-fire related CO poisoning associated with consumer products. More than 100 of those deaths are linked to portable generators.”

200 is not a rounding of 400. The CDC counts every unintentional, non-fire CO death; the CPSC counts only deaths tied to a consumer product — and, of those, singles out portable generators. A page that prints one figure without the other is reporting the choice it made, so both are printed.

Only the CPSC names the direction the exhaust must face. None of the three says the 20 feet is measured from the machine or from its exhaust, which is the ambiguity a person with a short driveway actually meets.

3 — Which symptoms to recognise

  • CDC: “The most common symptoms of CO poisoning are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, and confusion. CO symptoms are often described as “flu-like.””
  • CPSC: “Know the symptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning: headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, sleepiness, and confusion. If you suspect CO poisoning, get outside to fresh air immediately, and then call 911.”

The lists are close, not identical: the CDC names chest pain where the CPSC names sleepiness, and the CDC names upset stomach where the CPSC names nausea. A symptom one body names and the other does not is still a reason to leave the room, so the page prints the union and marks which body said which.

4 — When to replace the alarm

Only one of the three gives a number: the CDC says Replace your CO detector following the manufacturer's instructions or every 5 years. The CPSC and Ready.gov give placement and no interval. The page prints the 5-year figure as the CDC's, and says plainly that the label on your own detector wins.

Before anyone gets hurt: the alarm and the symptoms

Placement: the CDC says Install battery-operated or battery back-up CO detectors near every sleeping area in your home. The CPSC says Install battery-operated CO alarms or CO alarms with battery backup on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas. Interconnected CO alarms are best; when one sounds, they all sound. Ready.gov says Install carbon monoxide detectors with battery backup in central locations on every level of your home. One alarm is not the rule all three describe.
