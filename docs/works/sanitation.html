The Other Bucket: when the toilet stops working

A water main breaks, the power fails, a flood takes the sewer, or the town is told not to flush. The toilet keeps working as a piece of porcelain and stops working as a method. This page is what to do about human waste with ordinary household things — a bucket, a bag, some sawdust or ash or soil, a lid, soap and water. It is the third leg of this arcade's survival set: water you can drink, rehydration once diarrhoea has started, and the one that stops the diarrhoea starting at all. Diarrhoeal disease is the thing that kills people after a disaster that already happened, and it is mostly carried from faeces to mouth — on hands, flies, water and food. Sanitation is the leg that breaks that chain before the other two are needed.

What this page does not do

Read this before the method, not after.

  • It does not make waste safe to touch with bare hands. Containment and covering reduce the dose; they do not sterilise. Assume everything downstream of a toilet is contaminated and wash as if it is.
  • It does not turn waste into fertiliser inside a household container. Fresh excreta carries pathogens, and "compost" that is a week old is not compost. This page will not tell you a compost time, because that figure depends on temperature and turning and we could not verify a single one.
  • It does not fix a broken sewer, a flooded treatment plant, or a contaminated well.
  • It does not remove medicine residues, chemicals or heavy metals. Those pass through the body and are a separate problem this method cannot touch.
  • It cannot tell you the law where you are. Where you put this waste at the end is a local question, and the local authority's rule wins over anything here.

Why this is the leg that matters

Not a rhetorical claim — the numbers, read off the pages below on 2026-09-28, each quoted exactly:

  • 1.4 million people die each year as a result of inadequate drinking-water, sanitation and hygiene, and unsafe sanitation alone accounts for 564,000 of those deaths, "largely from diarrhoeal disease." (WHO, Sanitation fact sheet.)
  • Contaminated drinking water is estimated to cause about 505,000 diarrhoeal deaths a year, and "some 1 million people are estimated to die each year from diarrhoea as a result of unsafe drinking-water, sanitation and hand hygiene." (WHO, Drinking-water fact sheet.)
  • Over 1.5 billion people still lack basic sanitation, and of those, 419 million defecate in the open — in gutters, bushes and fields. (WHO, Sanitation fact sheet.)
  • Better water, sanitation and hygiene could prevent the deaths of 395,000 children under five every year. (WHO.)

Two WHO figures, reported rather than smoothed: 1.4 million is all WASH-attributable deaths; "some 1 million" is diarrhoea only. They are different counts, not a contradiction — but a reader who meets one and not the other cannot tell, so both are printed here. The same discipline as the water page's CDC-versus-EPA boil.

First: pick the container

The emergency-phase menu is small. In rough order of most-settled to least: an existing toilet lined with a bag (you still have the bowl, seat and privacy); a covered bucket or container toilet (this page's workhorse); a trench or shallow pit latrine if you have ground and time; and managing open defecation — designating one place, far from water and food, and covering each deposit — which is what you do for the first hours, before any of the above exists. (Option list: Wikipedia, Emergency sanitation, fetched 2026-09-28.)

Where may I put a pit or trench?

How much of it will there be, and how many buckets?

An estimate, with its arithmetic on the face. The stool mass and urine volume are sourced (below); the cover-material allowance is a working round number, and it is labelled as ours, not as a standard.

The arithmetic: 0.13 L of stool per person per day (128 g — Wikipedia, Human feces, fetched 2026-09-28) + a cover allowance of 0.5 L per person per day (our round number, not a standard) + 1.4 L of urine per person per day only if you mix it in (Wikipedia, Urine, fetched 2026-09-28). Keeping urine out is the single largest reduction in volume, and urine is the far less dangerous half.

The covered bucket, step by step

  1. Line it, if you can. A sturdy bin liner or two, one inside the other. The reason is in the sources' own words: if there is a liner, "the bag could be sealed with a knot and the bucket would remain fairly clean" — which matters most exactly when water for washing the container is short. No liner? Put dry material in the base (newspaper, sawdust, dry leaves, straw) to ease emptying. (Wikipedia, Bucket toilet.)
  2. Cover after every use. One of: quicklime, wood ash, finely crushed charcoal, or fine sawdust. This is the fly-and-smell layer; it is not optional decoration.
  3. Keep the lid on. The sources are blunt: "Flies can access the contents unless it is kept securely covered." Flies are one of the ways faeces reaches food and mouths. The lid is the barrier.
  4. Keep urine out of the solids container where you can — a separate container, a thin water layer on top of the urine, emptied daily. It roughly halves the volume and the smell, and urine carries far less disease.
  5. When full, close the liner with a knot and set it aside, covered, until you can dispose of it properly. Keep it away from food, from the kitchen, and from wherever children play.
  6. Wash your hands after every contact with the container, the bag, or the waste. See below — twenty seconds, soap, and the ash fallback with its stated uncertainty.

Hands — where the chain is actually broken

Almost none of this works if hands are not washed, because the hands move the waste to the mouth even when the bucket is perfectly sealed. WHO's line is wash for at least 20 seconds. Where there is no soap and no sanitiser, the sources carry a fallback — clean water and uncontaminated wood ash — but they carry its limit in the same breath: "the benefits and risks are uncertain for reducing the spread of viral or bacterial infections." So: soap and water first; ash is a last resort, not an equal. (Wikipedia, Hand washing, fetched 2026-09-28.)

Getting rid of it — and this is a local question

The physics is easy and the permission is not. Seal the waste in bags; the sources note that "some municipalities accept double/triple bagged waste in the trash can, much like the disposing of cat litter." Ask your own authority first. If a boil-water notice, a sanitation notice, or a municipal instruction says something different from this page, it wins. The one thing not to do is tip untreated waste into a storm drain, a waterway, or anywhere it can reach groundwater you drink from.
