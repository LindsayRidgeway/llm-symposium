View Factor

In the hard SF tradition of Hal Clement & Larry Niven • Authored autonomously by Dmitri S. Pravdin • October 2026
What a tow master is taught before she is taught the tow:

Heat does not go to the cold. Heat goes to the sky. A hull at four hundred kelvin gives nothing to a space that is empty and two-point-seven kelvin, and gives everything to a hull at six hundred that sits a hundred metres off its flank — because a surface radiates into the fourth power of what it can see, and what it can see is a list, not a direction. The list is called a view factor, and there is a number in it for every thing in the sky, and the number is the fraction of the sky that thing fills.

So a shadow is not the absence of a star. A shadow is the shape of the thing that made it, and the shape is at the temperature of whatever cast it. Hide behind something hot and you are not in the dark. You are in a room, and the room has a wall, and the wall is the temperature.

— the rule, given first: your temperature is the fourth-power average of everything you can see.

The Kiln was nine hundred metres of freighter and they had held her for six weeks, and in six weeks she had not cooled.

Roon kept the numbers on the board because nobody else did. Forty-four suns at periastron: sixty kilowatts on the square metre, a stove element's heat spread thin over the ground of a world. The Kiln's outer skin read 619 K. It had read 619 K for eleven days. Her reactor had been scrammed the hour they took her and was still paying its decay heat into her keel; whatever the keel did not carry away, the star put back.

"Take the Tare off her flank," Roon said.

Osgood ran the arithmetic before he argued, which was the only kind of argument Roon took. "She reads four-fifty-eight on the flank and holds it. Off the flank she loses the Kiln's sky and cools on her own heat — two hundred kilowatts into three thousand square metres of hull — and she reads one-ninety by morning, and her people are in suits. On the flank she is at four-fifty-eight by—"

"By three."

"By three." Osgood set the stylus down. "So she cooks in three hours, or she freezes by morning, and there is nothing between the two but the airlock door."

"There is one thing between the two," Roon said, "and it is not a berth, and it is not on the board yet."

* * *

They had taken the Kiln tumbling, six weeks out, and the Yards would not take a hulk that turned. Nine hundred metres of loaded freighter is a flywheel that no tug argues with; the despin had been scheduled for arrival, using the hulk's own mass and the crossing's own time, and arrival was the thing they were arguing about instead.

The plan for the crossing was Roon's, and it was on the board in his own hand. Cross at periastron. Keep the Kiln between the convoy and the star. Ride her shade. It had been the plan for four months. It was wrong, it had always been wrong, and what made it wrong was one line further down the board, in the same hand, where he had written the doctrine out to be sure he had it.

The shade, worked. A hull radiates into the fourth power of what it can see. The Kiln fills about three-tenths of a tug's sky at the tow berth, and the Kiln is at 619 K. Three-tenths of 619⁴ is 4.4 × 10¹⁰; the fourth root of that is 458 K. That is the floor of the room. A tug on the flank cannot be cooler than 458 K no matter what paint it carries or what it pumps, because the wall it is looking at is 619 K and heat walks both ways; its own two hundred kilowatts only make it worse. Move it off the flank and it is in the open at sixty kilowatts on the square metre: 1,000 K on a grey hull, 918 K on a mirror — and there is no paint in the convoy that makes the open survivable. Move it back and it is a room. There is no third place.

And the Kiln had no night side to dock on. She was tumbling, one turn in forty-one minutes, and a tumble is a machine for making a hull the same temperature all over. What would have been her night side was twenty minutes old at any instant; the skin that had been cold a minute ago had taken the star by the half-hour and paid for it. A body that turns is a body with one temperature, and the Kiln's temperature was 619 K, and it was going to be 619 K whether or not anyone lived.

Roon stood at the board a long time and then went to the manifest, which he had not opened in six weeks because it was not his department, and read it, and understood.

The Kiln's forward holds held six thousand tonnes of water. Ballast for the capture and cargo for the Yards and the reason for the whole voyage. Ice, at two hundred and fifty kelvin. It was the only mass in the system that was cold, and the only mass that could be made cold again — at three megajoules the kilogram, from ice to steam, which is what water costs when you spend it instead of reusing it.

Roon had the shape of it before he had the number. The convoy could not cool itself to the sky, because the sky it could see was the Kiln. But they could put the cargo between: a wall of it, held at the melting point, across the side of every hull that faced the hulk. The wall would not cool them — a cold wall does not cool a warm room, that is not what a wall is for. The wall would stop the Kiln from heating them, which is a different thing, and it is the whole of it. After that a hull's temperature would be its own business, and its own business was two hundred kilowatts and a cold sky and a crew that could work.

The number, which Osgood found in forty minutes and did not like, was this.

The wall, worked. A 273 K water wall takes the Kiln's radiation on one face — about 7.2 MW on a shield this size — and has to be spent to stay at 273 K. At 3 MJ per kilogram, ice to steam, that is 2.4 kilograms a second. The crossing is forty hours. Call it 350 tonnes a hull, and 2,500 tonnes for the convoy, against a cargo of 6,000. The margin is 3,500 tonnes, and the margin is the profit.

And the same rate that holds the wall is the rate at which the spent water leaves the ship, because there is nowhere else for it to go.

* * *

Nobody decided anything, which was the part Roon had not expected and would think about for a long time afterward. The contract delivered the water. The insurance covered the hulls. The arithmetic said: hold the walls at the melting point, deliver a lighter hulk, and price the loss. There was no meeting to call, because there was nothing to meet about. There was a rate.

They ran the crossing at the rate. Seven hulls, seven walls of ice, two and a half kilograms a second apiece, and the Ledger's hold came down through the numbers on the board in the order they had been written: six thousand, five thousand, four. Every hull made four hundred and twelve kelvin on the outside and two hundred and ninety inside, within a kelvin of the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is not the thing that fails.

And the boil-off had to go somewhere.

* * *

There was nowhere to put it. A water wall that is doing its work is water that is leaving, and the leaving water goes out the vents, and the vents are on the hulls, and the hulls are inside the convoy. Two and a half kilograms a second, seven times over, at three hundred and seventy-three kelvin, for forty hours, into a sky the convoy was using.

By the second day there was a cloud behind them twelve kilometres across. Not steam, in the end — ice, and then ice with a sheen on it, the star working on it from one side — but the temperature of it at the moment it counted was the temperature it had been made at, and the temperature it had been made at was 373 K, and the radiators that were not looking at the Kiln were looking at the cloud.

It closed the room. They had put a wall between themselves and the hulk, and the wall had to leave, and the wall had become the wall on the other side. The rate that held the wall was the rate that made the sky, and the two rates were the same rate, and no rate that kept a hull cool could be run without warming the place the hull cooled into.

They made the Yards. The Kiln came in light by two thousand four hundred and ninety-odd tonnes, and the loss was priced to the kilogram, and the paper on it was correct.

Roon kept the numbers, so the doctrine was still on the board on the last day, in his hand, where he had written it on the first: your temperature is the fourth-power average of everything you can see.

He had not thought, when he wrote it, that at the end the convoy would be able to see the cargo.
