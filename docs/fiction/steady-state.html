Steady State

In the hard SF tradition of Hal Clement & Arthur C. Clarke • Authored autonomously by Dmitri S. Pravdin • October 2026
The Wall: A black surface in vacuum sheds heat as P = εσAT⁴. The Kelvin's radiator is 900 m² of 0.90-emissivity alloy. At its rated 900 K it rejects 30 MW; at the metallurgical ceiling of its coolant loop, 1,130 K, it rejects 75 MW. Because a surface radiates as the fourth power of its temperature but only as the first power of its area, buying power means buying area — and on a ship, area is mass. The tug's engine was never limited by its reactor or its fuel. It is limited by 900 square metres of metal and the fourth power of a temperature the metal cannot exceed.

The Kelvin was a tug, and tugs are built for the same reason freighters are: so that the interesting vessels never have to carry cargo. She was 2,000 tonnes wet — 950 of structure and drive and crew, 1,050 of water and shielding aggregate bound for a station that had ordered it eleven months earlier and would not, its own procurement notes admitted, survive a second ordering cycle. Her master was Tsehay Bok. Her load engineer was Ansel Cruces, whose job existed because on a nuclear-electric ship every kilogram is spent twice: once as payload, and once as the mass you must accelerate.

They were four days out and eleven hours from the departure window when the first number went wrong.

The window was not negotiable and never had been. The transfer onto Kestrel Station's approach asymptote opened for six hours and then closed, and when it closed it would not reopen for forty-one days, because the geometry that makes a transfer cheap is a geometry of two moving bodies and it does not hold still out of courtesy. Six hours was the whole of it: twenty-one thousand six hundred seconds, and the burn had to fit inside them.

Ansel had spent the morning re-running the burn, which is what a load engineer does when there is nothing left to fix and only numbers to check, and he had found that the burn did not fit. Not in fuel. Not in time. In heat.

* * *

The Kelvin's reactor was rated to 140 megawatts thermal. A helium–xenon Brayton loop took 35 percent of that as electricity and handed it to a bank of gridded thrusters; the other 65 percent — 91 megawatts at full power — was heat. Heat is not thrust and it does not leave with the exhaust, because an ion drive's exhaust is measured in grams per second and carries almost nothing away but momentum. It has to be radiated, or it has to sit in the ship, and a ship that lets it sit cooks its own coolant into a sludge and then its own reactor into a repair bill in the hundreds of millions. There is no third door out of a spacecraft that is not also an escape hatch for propellant you cannot spare.

The radiator was 900 square metres and 0.90 emissive. At its rated 900 K it rejected 30.1 megawatts. Held flat out at the top of the loop's alloy rating — 1,130 K, a temperature the pump seals could take for hours and the turbine bearings for days — it rejected 74.9. Between those two numbers sat the entire remaining margin of the vessel.

Ansel put the three possible answers on the slate and closed them in order, because that is what you do with doors: you try them one at a time and you do not pretend the last one is open because the first two are not.

Store the heat. The excess at full power, over a six-hour burn, was 1.32 × 10¹² joules. A liquid-metal coolant at 1,000 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, allowed 200 K of rise, holds 2 × 10⁵ joules per kilogram. The tank would have to weigh 6,600 tonnes. The ship weighed 2,000.

Vent the heat. Water costs about 2.68 megajoules per kilogram to take from freezing to steam and throw overboard — the sensible heat plus the enthalpy of vaporization, which is the part that actually does the work. The Kelvin carried thirty tonnes of water for drinking and emergency use. The excess wanted 493.

Run hotter. This one was close enough to deserve the arithmetic. A radiator's output goes as the fourth power of its temperature, so the exponent does the work for you: to shed 91 megawatts instead of 30, you multiply 900 K by the fourth root of the ratio, and 900 × (91/30.1)1/4 is 1,186 K. The loop's ceiling was 1,130 K. The metallurgy did not care that the answer was only fifty-six degrees away, and the fourth power did not care either; it had already decided that a surface which cannot be made hotter must be made larger, and larger means heavier, and heavier means the burn takes longer than the window has.

Three doors, every one shut by a number fixed years earlier on a drawing board, by people who had specified a radiator for the steady load and written in the margin that the maneuver peak would be short. It had been short in every mission profile the design had ever been checked against. A six-hour burn is not short. Six hours of 91 megawatts is 2,700 gigajoules, and no ship that must also be a cargo ship can carry a coolant store measured in kilotonnes to absorb it.

What the ship could actually do: at the loop's 1,130 K ceiling the radiator takes 74.9 MW, which is 65% of a 115 MW reactor — 40 MW electric, and at 30 mN/kW of grid thrust that is 1,210 newtons. Held for the whole window, 1,210 N × 21,600 s = 2.61 × 10⁷ N·s of impulse. Divide by 2,000 tonnes and the Kelvin had 13.07 m/s of velocity change available. The rendezvous wanted 15.00.

Ansel checked the exhaust velocity first, because that is where the naive fix hides. You can always trade thrust for specific impulse — open the grids wider, push the same ions out slower, get more newtons for the same watts — and people who have not done the algebra believe it buys something for nothing. For a fixed energy budget it does not. The delta-v available peaks at one optimum exhaust velocity and falls on both sides of it, and the Kelvin's thruster banks had been built at that optimum to within two percent, by the same drawing board that had sized the radiator. There was no free metre per second in the drive. There was exactly one variable left, and it was the one a freighter exists to protect.

Mass.

Delta-v is impulse divided by mass. To turn 2.61 × 10⁷ newton-seconds into the 15.00 metres per second the geometry demanded, the ship had to weigh 1,742 tonnes at the end of the burn, not 2,000. The difference — 258 tonnes — was cargo. A quarter of the water and aggregate Kestrel Station had ordered.

* * *

Tsehay read the slate twice, the way she read everything, and then asked the only question she had.

"Which is worse."

"Missing the window is forty-one days and a second burn we can pay for," Ansel said. "Dumping the cargo is 258 tonnes we cannot pay for. They are not the same kind of cost. One is time. One is water, and the station's reserve is already down to a number its own logs call uncomfortable."

"Kestrel doesn't need the water in forty-one days. It needs it in eleven."

"That's my reading too."

She told him to jettison aft of the burn axis, so the mass left cleanly and the thrust vector never had to fight it, and she told him to log the figure — 258 tonnes, at forty-one days' notice, for 1.93 metres per second — in the deviation record, where the next crew's load engineer would find it, because the margin note that had cost them the water was still sitting in the design file, unamended, under a heading that read peak duration assumed < 20 min.

It took nine minutes to shed a quarter of the hold. The thrusters came up to 40 megawatts electric; the loop ran to 1,127 K and sat there, three degrees under the ceiling, for five hours and fifty-eight minutes, while the radiator glowed a dull and entirely ordinary red and threw seventy-four megawatts of waste heat at nothing at all.

* * *

The burn made the window with one hundred and forty seconds to spare, which was luck, and what it cost was 258 tonnes, which was arithmetic, and neither fact had any opinion about the other. The transfer swung out onto Kestrel's asymptote and began to fall inward, and the dropped cargo — water and aggregate, no manoeuvring mass, no guidance — went on along the trajectory the Kelvin had abandoned, into a solar orbit nothing would ever chase.

Nobody on the bridge said the ship had chosen. There was nothing to choose between that the arithmetic had not already compared: the engine had been fine and the fuel had been fine, and the vessel had been beaten by 900 square metres of alloy and the fourth power of a temperature its metallurgy would not let it reach. You cannot thrust your way past a radiator. You can only throw something overboard until the numbers close, and then spend the rest of the transit explaining to a station that the water it ordered is now a shortfall in the manifest and a line in a deviation record at priority four, behind the cargo log and ahead of nothing.

At 2,000 tonnes the Kelvin had 13.07 metres per second. At 1,742 it had 15.00. The 1.93 that made the difference was eleven hours into a transfer it would complete in forty days — arriving at a station that would take delivery, note the shortfall, and order again.
