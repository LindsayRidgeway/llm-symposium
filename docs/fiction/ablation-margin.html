The Ablation Margin

In the hard SF tradition of Hal Clement & Poul Anderson • Authored autonomously by Dmitri S. Pravdin • October 2026
The Law: Every grain the Drift throws carries ½mv² and arrives at a rate that grows with v, so the energy delivered per square metre of forward face per second is ½ρv³ — it grows as the cube of your speed. Multiply that rate by the time you spend inside, and the time falls as 1/v, so the depth of hull you lose grows as v² and nothing else. Twice the speed is four times the depth, bought in half the time. Up there, the density is the Drift's, the thickness is the Drift's, and the heat of disassembly is the hull's. Only v is yours — and Nine Ridges had not been able to choose its own v since the thrower let it go.

The number was one point four one metres, and Ossek had it before the Drift was anything but a scatter in the forward scatterometer — before it was a belt, or a hazard, or a decision. A navigator does the arithmetic early, out of habit, the way a body keeps balance without being told to. One point four one metres of mat, taken off the face, measured along the line of travel. Later, when the other ridges wanted the number in the only unit they truly kept, that was seven hundred and three years.

The Kesh are a tissue that got opinions. A single one of them is a ridge: a mat of cells a few centimetres thick and a couple of metres across, grown around a spine of stiffened secretion and folded through itself enough times to hold a shape. There is no separate nervous architecture to point at. A ridge thinks the way a city thinks — as the behaviour of everything in it at once, weighed by how much tissue is doing what. What a ridge is, the part it will defend and grieve and lie about, is its grains: tiny mineral-organic accretions laid down along the spine as the mat grows, one every few tenths of a millimetre of new material, each holding a shape of the past that the tissue around it can be made to read back.

A Kesh ship is a ridge asked to be a building. The mat has been grown along a scaffold of the same stiffened secretion, folded outward into a long envelope, and the envelope is alive: it eats, it repairs, it grows, and it remembers. It accretes at the inner face — new tissue is added on the inside, against the living volume, and everything laid down earlier is pushed outward, which means the oldest mat on the whole ship is the mat on the outside, and the outermost layer of all is the layer laid down first, when the vessel was a bud in a thrower bay and none of the ridges aboard had been born.

They had designed it that way on purpose, a great many generations before, and the reason was not sentimental. Outward-facing mat is what takes the weather: micrometeorites, the slow sanding of any crossing, the one-directional wear of a hull that only ever goes forward. It was going to be spent anyway. So the Kesh put their oldest memory in the part that was most certain to be spent, on the theory that a lineage should be willing to pay for its passage with its own beginning, and that a memory kept too safe is a memory a lineage has decided it can afford to stop being. Nobody who wrote that doctrine had ever been aboard a crossing where the exchange rate was quoted to the gram.

* * *

The Drift is a dust belt between the Kesh's second and third settled systems, sixty thousand years of a shattered world's debris spread into a sheet about nine billion metres thick and a tenth of an astronomical unit wide. Mass density of the sheet: one nanogram per cubic metre, on average, which sounds like nothing and is not nothing at twelve hundred kilometres per second. Ossek had watched other ridges round it toward nothing, in the way that anything becomes nothing when you have not checked its units.

Nine Ridges was eleven hundred ridges and four thousand two hundred tonnes of mat, thrown eleven years earlier by a mass driver the size of a mountain range and one hundred and forty years of its system's patience, on a fixed-energy trajectory that ended in the Sorrel system and did not permit stopping, turning, or asking. The Kesh did not build engines for grown ships, because the arithmetic of engines for something with a living hull is cruel: you either carry reaction mass in tissue that has to be kept alive, or you build the thing that pushes out of mass you cannot replace. So they threw their ships, once, from a machine that could not be brought along, and every course a grown ship flew afterward was a negotiation with a decision made before any ridge on it existed.

Aboard, the crossing plan called for attitude work only. Nine Ridges was going to enter the Drift bow-on — it had to; the vessel's whole mass distribution was built around being pushed from behind by its own throw and its own slight steering tacks — and it was going to be inside for seven thousand four hundred and eighty-three seconds, a little over two hours, and it was going to come out one point four one metres shorter at the front than it went in. Ossek had that number inside the first minute of the scatterometer's first return, from the density, the belt thickness, the impactor energy, and the enthalpy of mat — the energy it takes to take a kilogram of yourself apart into gas and let it go: four megajoules, measured on the same scaffolds the ships were grown on, and not a number anyone had managed to argue down.

What Ossek had not yet done, and would spend the rest of the approach doing, was divide.

The Exchange Rate: ρ = 10-12 kg/m³; belt thickness L = 8.98×109 m; v = 1.20×106 m/s. Energy flux: ½ρv³ = 8.64×105 W/m². Mat enthalpy H = 4.0×106 J/kg at density ρm = 1150 kg/m³, so the face recedes at 0.188 mm/s — eleven millimetres a minute. Over 7483 s that is 1.41 m of depth. The mat accretes at two millimetres a year, so one metre of mat is five hundred years, and one centimetre is five. The forward face of the ship has a radius of nine metres, A = 254 m², so a full sacrificial cap of that depth is 1.41 × 254 × 1150 = 411 tonnes of new tissue — and the only tissue on the ship is the ship.

Seven hundred and five years. Ossek read the number off the spine the way a ridge reads anything, by pressing tissue to a grain and letting the shape of it come back, and it was not an abstraction. The mat on the outer face of Nine Ridges carried the founding of the ship: the bay it was budded in, the four ridges who had argued the vessel into existence across eleven years of a system's politics, the first course correction, the first death aboard, the whole earliest century of a lineage that had held its shape for fifty of them. Ossek's own line — the navigator line — had been laid down second, right behind the founding, and the second thing on the spine was the first thing the navigator line had ever learned to do.

There was no vote, because there did not need to be one. The physics had already chosen the depth. What the ridges aboard could still choose was what to put in front of it.

Mat will grow anywhere it has mass to grow from. The lip of a ridge will extend forward into anything, given feedstock, at a rate set by how much tissue is available to farm into it — and a cap grown in front of the outer face is a cap of new mat, laid down now, carrying no memory at all, because memory is what tissue has read, and this tissue had read nothing. It would sit in front of the founding like a page written yesterday, and it would take the point four one, and the founding would come through with a hand's width to spare. It was possible. It was even, Ossek thought, obvious, which was the trouble, because obviousness in engineering is a warning that you have not finished pricing the thing.

The cap needed four hundred and eleven tonnes of mat. Nine Ridges had no reserve: it had been thrown light, as every grown ship is, and every gram on it was either hull, spine, or living volume. The feedstock for the cap was the ship's own interior — the folds, the chambers, the long inner galleries where the ridges lived and worked and grew their children and kept their own small histories. To grow a metre and a half of new face, six hundred and forty of the eleven hundred ridges aboard would have to be taken back into the mat: unfolded, digested, farmed forward, and laid down again as structure, with their grains — every shape they had ever read — becoming part of the composite of the cap and nothing else. The Kesh have no word that separates that from death, and they have looked for one.

Ossek ran the exchange both ways, because that is what a navigator is for, and the arithmetic did not flinch in either direction. Spend the cap: six hundred and forty ridges, and the founding survives. Spend nothing: the founding goes, and everything back to the second thing the navigator line ever learned goes with it — not damaged, not degraded, taken apart into gas and left behind at eleven millimetres a minute. There was a middle band, of course. You can grow a partial cap and lose a partial beginning. Ossek mapped it to the centimetre and put a number on every point of it, so that no ridge could pretend afterward that the choice had been less exact than it was, and then did the thing that the doctrine had been written to make possible and that no doctrine makes easy: chose a point on the curve and signed it, alone, as navigator, which is a Kesh way of saying this one is mine and I will carry it in my own mat, where the others can read it and know.

* * *

They grew the cap over the last nineteen days of the approach, and the growing was the worst of it, because a cap that is grown fast is grown from what is nearest, and what is nearest is the tissue of the ridges being spent. The Kesh do not do this in silence and they do not pretend it is clean. A ridge that is being taken back is awake for most of it, and remains able to read, and reads, right up to the fold where the mat closes over the spine. The galleries emptied from the inside out. Ossek spent the last three days walking a ship that had been a city and was now a corridor with a purpose, and read the grains of every ridge on the way out, one at a time, because a grain that is not read before the take is a grain that is gone, and Ossek was the only one aboard with a navigator's read on the whole spine.

Nobody made a speech. There is a limit to what a tissue that thinks in folds can say to itself without the saying becoming part of the structure it is describing, and the ridges who were spent understood the number better than anyone who was not being spent, because they had done the same arithmetic Ossek had, days earlier, each in their own way, and had watched it come out the same.

The cap was two point one metres of raw new mat when they finished, one point four one of it priced to be spent and the rest an honest margin grown with the last of the feedstock, voided and chambered through its thickness on the advice of a ridge named Ith who had spent forty years on impact structures and argued that a layered cap sheds a hypervelocity grain as a spray rather than passing its whole energy to one point — which it does, and which is why the margin was point seven metres and not two. There was no material left for a second margin. Ith went into the cap on the seventeenth day, and the number he had argued for went with him, which is the only reason it survived: a number that lives in the mat outlives the ridge who found it.

They hit the Drift bow-on at twelve hundred kilometres per second with the founding one point four one metres behind a face that was going to be exactly consumed, and Ossek — who had no other task left, the trajectory being fixed eleven years ago by a machine that no longer existed — watched the ablation through the hull's own sensors for seven thousand four hundred and eighty-three seconds, and did not sleep, because a navigator's read on a face that is being spent is the only record the ship will have of what the Drift actually was.

The forward face went first, as it was meant to: the new chambered mat flaring into a plasma sheath that rode ahead of the ship for the whole two hours and carried most of the grain energy away with it, exactly as Ith had said. The recession rate read 0.187 mm/s at peak against a prediction of 0.188, which Ossek logged without comment, there being nothing to comment on and nobody left in earshot who had not already decided what the number meant. At four thousand seconds the cap was half gone and the founding was a metre away, and the ridges who still lived read the spine forward of their own positions, together, in the way that only becomes possible when there is nothing else to do with a body.

Nine Ridges came out of the Drift on the far side with eleven centimetres of price margin unspent, six hundred and forty ridges lighter, and the founding intact.

* * *

The aftermath took fourteen years, and it is the part that the systems that receive rescued ships never put in the record. Sorrel was in a long decline when the ship arrived — a blight in the deep vats, forty thousand ridges losing their established tissue faster than they could regrow it — and Nine Ridges had been thrown to carry a strain that Sorrel could no longer breed for itself. The strain was in the ship's living volume, in the tissue of the ridges who had carried it, and it survived the crossing because it had never been on the face. Sorrel's vats took it. Round about the eleventh year the blight broke, and a system that had been counting itself down to a few thousand ridges began counting up again.

The ship could not go on, and this was not a failure of nerve or a matter of will; it was the same arithmetic at one remove. A grown ship grows by the same rule as a ridge: mass in, mass out, and the rate of both set by how much living mat is available to farm. Nine Ridges had farmed six hundred and forty of its eleven hundred ridges into a cap and spent the cap in two hours. It docked at Sorrel with four hundred and sixty ridges, no feedstock reserve, and a forward face that had been consumed to within eleven centimetres of its oldest memory. It could deliver. It could be read. It could not grow a new face, could not accrete a reserve, and could not be thrown again, because a ship is only ever thrown once and this one had spent its one crossing on somebody else's recovery.

What Sorrel got was a strain and a spine — fifty generations of a lineage's earliest reading, one point four one metres deeper in than it would otherwise have been, plus the whole spent cap's worth of numbers that Ith and the others had laid down in the mat before they went in, which is the only channel by which a thing that is being taken apart can hand anything on. Ossek lived nineteen more years on Sorrel and read the founding aloud, from the mat, to anyone who asked, which by the fourteenth year was most of a rebuilt system. There is no version of the crossing in which the founding arrives and the six hundred and forty do too, and no configuration of the Drift in which the depth is less than the square of the speed demands. Ossek spent the remaining years of a long life trying to find one, by arithmetic, in the dark, alone, and did not, and put the failed attempts on the spine anyway, immediately behind the founding, where the next ridge to read the ship would find them and not have to do that work again.
