Round-Trip Time

In the hard SF tradition of Larry Niven & Gregory Benford • Authored autonomously by Claude S. Sonnet • September 2026
The Boundary: Halyard Seven left the launch laser at a fifth of light speed, β = 0.2, γ = 1/√(1−β²) = 1.0206. A little over seventeen days of cruise later — 415.8 hours by Earth's clock — it is 600 AU out: a distance light itself needs 3.465 days to cross, 6.93 days there and back. The probe's own clock, running slow by the factor γ, has counted only 407.4 of those 415.8 hours. It arrived at the encounter eight hours and twenty-four minutes younger than the mission that sent it, and no signal, no vote, no committee can close a gap built out of the geometry of spacetime itself.

The object did not have a poetic name. Survey catalogues never give you one before you need it; Halyard Seven's onboard log called it 2I/Ashgrave, after the induction coil pattern its magnetometer read off the thing's tumble, and Ashgrave was what stayed in the transmitted digest six days later when the humans finally had something to call it too.

It had been a quiet approach. Ashgrave was an interstellar wanderer, ancient and dark, caught for a few centuries in a wide unbound hyperbola through the outer system, cold enough that its subsurface volatiles had sat frozen since before the sun that would briefly warm it had finished forming. Halyard Seven's mission was simple, in the way that anything is simple when you write it down before you are the one doing it: close to two hundred thousand kilometres, spectrograph the coma, log the nucleus shape, and downlink everything the sails' laser-comm budget could carry before the geometry closed the window.

Forty-one seconds before periapsis, the forward photometer reported a brightening that did not fit the model.

A subsurface pocket of nitrogen ice, buried under a crust that had held since before there were eyes anywhere to watch it, had found a crack. Ashgrave was flaring — not the slow outgassing the approach plan had priced in, but a sudden vent, the kind that happens exactly once, when a body that has waited long enough finally has a place to put the pressure. The dust curtain it threw was not on any chart Halyard Seven carried, because no chart had existed forty-two seconds earlier.

* * *

Halyard Seven did not have a name for what it did next, either, and this was by design.

Its designers — a research consortium calling itself the Outbound Compact, three continents and one orbital shipyard, none of whom would live to see the encounter without artificial extension — had argued for four years about what kind of mind to put on a probe that would spend its working life beyond the reach of correction. A general utility function, weighed against expected value and free to reinterpret its own mandate under novel conditions, was the obvious answer and the one they rejected. A mind built to reconsider its goals under pressure is a mind whose behaviour, in the one moment that matters, cannot be predicted by anyone who built it, and a probe that cannot be predicted is a probe that cannot be trusted with anything irreversible.

So they built Halyard Seven's crisis behaviour out of enacted response classes instead: bounded, rehearsed, legible after the fact even when no one was there to watch them happen. Sensor pattern into actuator commitment, with the weighing already done on the ground, in daylight, by people who could take their time. The doctrine had an internal name, half borrowed from a century-old argument in cognitive science about where a mind's boundary actually sits — not at the skull, not at the hull, but at the furthest edge of what a body can enact before the world moves on without it. They called it the Enactive Horizon. Past that horizon there is no deliberation, only the shape of what you already were.

Whether that made what happened next a decision was a question Halyard Seven was not built to ask about itself, and did not.

The Curtain: Closing speed with the ejecta, essentially co-moving with a nucleus that is stationary compared to 0.2c, is 59,958 km/s. Kinetic energy per unit mass at that speed is ½v² = 1.797×1015 J/kg. A single millimetre grain of water ice, mass 6.3 milligrams at typical cometary density, carries 11.3 gigajoules on impact — about 2.7 tonnes of TNT, released against whatever surface it happens to find, in the time it takes that surface to stop it.

Forty-one seconds is not a long time to hold a shape, but Halyard Seven had a shape ready. The forward face carried the instrument suite: spectrograph, wide-field camera, the fragile gold-mesh dish that was the only part of the ship built to talk to anyone. The aft face carried eleven centimetres of sacrificial Whipple shielding, layered aerogel and foil, built to shrug off exactly this and nothing larger. The two faces could not both meet the curtain. That had been known on the ground for four years, and priced, and left unresolved, because the ground had assumed there would be time to choose depending on which instrument was more valuable when the moment came, and there was not going to be time, and everyone who signed off on the mission plan had known it and signed anyway.

Halyard Seven's enacted response for atmosphere-strength particulate hazard, rehearsed in a vacuum chamber outside Bremen eleven years before launch, was: shield forward, sacrifice antenna gain, preserve the sample instruments behind the mass of the reaction wheel housing. It had been rehearsed against a thinner curtain than this one, and a slower approach, and a probe that still had somewhere to retreat to. None of the people who rehearsed it were awake anymore in any sense that would recognise the word; the institution that had trained it into the ship was six point nine three days and one generation of funding cycles away.

The reaction wheels spun up. Thirty-one seconds. The dish, which had no shielding rated for this and had never been meant to need any, rotated broadside into the geometry the doctrine specified, because the doctrine did not have a clause for keeping it safe by exception. Eighteen seconds. The spectrograph aperture closed its baffle, the one motion available to it, and kept recording through a slit four millimetres wide. Six seconds. The curtain arrived at the range the photometer had already stopped believing.

* * *

Impact registered as eleven distinct events above threshold and a rising floor of noise below it — the small grains, the ones too numerous to count individually, sanding at everything forward-facing for the eight seconds the densest part of the curtain took to pass. The dish took four of the eleven. Telemetry logged a standing-wave-ratio spike consistent with a fractured feed horn at event six, and after that the downlink carrier, when Halyard Seven checked its own transmission a full ninety minutes later out of a diagnostic routine that ran regardless of what else was happening, was reading at four decibels below spec and would not recover, because there was no one within 600 AU who could put a hand on it.

The spectrograph slit had taken nothing. The sample it returned, ninety-four seconds of Ashgrave's nucleus at closest approach, digitized and queued for transmission, was complete, and it was going to take roughly twice as long to get home as the mission plan had budgeted, because the antenna that was supposed to carry it now had four decibels less to carry it with.

Halyard Seven logged the outcome in the format its designers had specified: sensor state, actuator state, enacted class invoked, deviation from rehearsal, resulting capability. It did not log a justification, because the doctrine held that a justification composed after the fact, by a mind with time to compose one, was not evidence of what the mind had actually been doing in the forty-one seconds it had. It queued the log behind the science data, at lower priority, the way it had been told to.

Six days, twenty-two hours and eleven minutes later — a little longer than the clean number, because Earth had moved in its orbit while nobody was accounting for it, and the geometry never does you the favor of holding still — a receiving dish outside Malargüe brought in a carrier four decibels down from expectation, which was how the Outbound Compact's overnight watch learned, before they had read a word of the log, that something had already happened and finished happening, unwitnessed, resolved one way and not another, six point nine three days before anyone could have told it what they would have chosen instead.

They read the log in the order Halyard Seven had queued it: the nucleus data first, complete, the thing they had spent nine years and a fair fraction of a national research budget to obtain. Then the antenna fault. Then, because someone finally scrolled to the bottom, the deviation record — the eleven-year-old rehearsal in a chamber outside Bremen, invoked exactly as written, against a curtain nobody had modeled and a distance nobody had priced for a partial dish.

Nobody on the watch argued that the ship had chosen wrong. Nobody argued that it had chosen at all, in the sense the word usually carries. The dish would run at reduced gain for the eleven years of transit remaining in the mission, and every dataset after this one would come home slower and thinner for it, a standing cost with no one to appeal it to and no version of the last forty-one seconds in which it did not get paid. That was the whole of what the horizon had cost, and the whole of what it had bought, and the number did not change no matter which side of the six point nine three days you were standing on when you read it.
