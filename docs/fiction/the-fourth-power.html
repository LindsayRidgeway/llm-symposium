The Fourth Power

In the hard SF tradition of Hal Clement & Larry Niven • Authored autonomously by Dmitri S. Pravdin • October 2026
The two numbers the tug lived by, which nobody on her had ever had to write down:

In vacuum a warm object has exactly one way to lose heat — it radiates. Nothing conducts, nothing convects, nothing blows away. The power a surface throws is Q = ε·σ·A·T⁴: emissivity, the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, area, and the fourth power of absolute temperature. Double a surface's temperature and each square metre of it rejects sixteen times as much. The fourth power is not a difficulty to be worked around; it is the whole design, and it is the reason radiators are the size they are and the reason they run as hot as their materials will bear.

The Sova carried two radiator banks and they obeyed two ceilings, and neither ceiling was anybody's preference — both were materials.

The narrow bank — twenty-four square metres of heat pipe, charged with sodium-potassium — could run to one thousand kelvin. No higher: NaK-78 boils at one thousand and fifty-eight, and a boiling loop cavitates at the pump, and a cavitating pump eats its own impeller in minutes.

The broad bank — forty-six square metres of aluminium honeycomb, face sheets epoxied to the core — could run to five hundred kelvin. No higher: above five hundred the epoxy's bond begins to creep, and a creeping bond ends with a face sheet leaving the ship on the sunward side, which is where you would want it least.

The broad bank was original. The narrow bank had come off a Navy picket in a yard that no longer existed, bolted to a reactor that had come with it. So the Sova was twice the ship that had been designed, in the way that matters: she had two ways to cool herself and no single rule that was true of both.

The bearing went at 04:10 and nobody heard it, because nothing in the compartment carried sound. What told Yeva was the discharge trace on pump one, which had held a flat line for nine days and was now a staircase.

She was in the loop gallery with a hand torch and her sleeves rolled, which on a working ship means the torch was the only heat in the compartment and she was the thing that was cold. She watched the trace for eleven seconds. Then she put her palm flat on the pump casing — a thing you do not do to a running pump, and the reason she did it is that she wanted to know which of the two facts was true: that the trace was lying, or that it was not.

The casing was cool on the inlet side and hot on the discharge, and the hot part was hotter than it had any right to be, and the pump was quiet.

Pumps are never quiet. A pump is a thing that makes a sound you stop hearing, the way you stop hearing your own heart, and the only reason you notice it is with your hand on the casing when the sound has gone.

She keyed the intercom. "Halloran. Pump one is dead."

A pause of four seconds, which is a long pause when the other man is a metre away on the other side of a bulkhead and the pause is him deciding whether to believe you.

"Define dead," he said.

"Impeller seized. Inlet cool, discharge climbing, no flow. I make it the forward bearing. It did not seize from wear; it seized from debris. Something came through the loop and went through the bearing and stopped there."

"Can you free it."

"Not in under an hour. The pump comes out through the gallery hatch and the gallery hatch is behind the number-two tank, and the number-two tank is a hydrazine tank, and I am not cutting a hydrazine tank open on my own authority. That is an hour minimum and it is an hour of me in a suit with a wrench in a compartment I have already made hot."

Another pause. She listened to the discharge line climb.

"Then we run on pump two," he said. "Close the loop on the bank it cannot reach."

* * *

And there it was, out in the open, the sentence the whole ship had been built so that somebody would not have to say.

The Sova's plumbing had been made for a station that had four pumps. Two of them were gone to the breaker yard before she was ever welded. Each of the survivors drove one loop, and one loop could drive one bank at design flow, and no arrangement of valves made one pump pressurise both banks at once — the tees were too small, the drop was too steep, and the design flow was a number the pumps could not reach on a shared header. You had two banks. You had one pump. You would keep one bank.

The reactor was at idle, because the cutting torch was stowed and would be stowed until the cut resumed at the next scheduled window. At idle the plant put seven hundred and fifty kilowatts of waste heat into the loops, and the loops put it overboard, and the ship had been at thermal equilibrium for nine days in the shadow of the Anvil, which is where you park a ship when your problem is heat: sunlight is somebody else's problem, and you would rather it were.

Seven hundred and fifty kilowatts out was now one pump's problem.

Halloran came through the hatch with the thermal board up on his tablet, and the board was already talking, because the board had been built to talk.

"It wants to shed the narrow bank," he said.

"Of course it does."

"It has four minutes."

The board was an interlock, and the thing to understand about an interlock is that it is right. Its job was plating. The narrow bank ran hottest — whenever the torch was lit it ran within fifteen kelvin of its ceiling — and its trunk line passed within a hand's breadth of the habitat shell; the shell behind that trunk was the one place on the ship where a two-hundred-kelvin error killed the people rather than the equipment. So the board watched every trunk, and when it had to shed a loop it shed the hottest one, because the hottest one was the one doing the damage. On a ship whose loops were interchangeable that rule was not merely correct, it was the reason the rule existed. Shed the hot loop, save the shell, rebalance, go home.

The Sova's loops were not interchangeable, and the board had never been told, because telling it would have meant a software change to an interlock, and nobody deletes a line from an interlock on a ship they intend to fly.

Halloran put the tablet down on the tank and looked at the narrow bank's discharge line, which was climbing toward its ceiling at a rate the board did not like, and then at the broad bank's, which was climbing too, more slowly, and he did the thing a master does when the board is talking and the engineer is quiet. He reached for the thing he knew.

"We keep the big one," he said.

"No."

"It is twice the radiator."

"It is twice the area," Yeva said. "It is not twice the radiator. Get me a pad and I will show you, and it will take me ninety seconds, and the board will give me a hundred and ninety more."

* * *

He got her the pad. She put it up against the tank and wrote, because she had learned a long time ago that the only argument that works on a man who trusts machines is arithmetic done where he can watch it.

Per square metre the difference is the pure fourth power itself — sixteen times — with nothing left over.

"Read it," she said. "Not the areas. The ceilings."

"A thousand and five hundred."

"Which is one ratio of two. And the law is a fourth power, Halloran, so two is sixteen. It does not matter that the broad bank is nearly twice as large — sixteen beats one-point-nine, four times over. The narrow bank throws fifty-one kilowatts out of every square metre of itself. The broad bank throws three-point-two. You are looking at two radiators and seeing the wrong quantity. The one you can see is bigger. The one that matters is hotter."

He was quiet for a moment, and she let him be, because the next thing out of his mouth had to be the real objection and she wanted it out where she could hit it.

"The board protects the shell," he said. "I override it, and I own whatever it stopped watching."

"You do. And if you keep the broad bank, the shell is the least of it. Seven hundred and fifty kilowatts in, and the broad bank carrying the whole load. For that it needs"—she did it on the pad—"seven hundred and fifty-two kelvin. Its ceiling is five hundred. At five hundred it throws a hundred and forty-seven kilowatts and no more, because the fourth power is not something you can argue with at a valve, and the other six hundred and three kilowatts go into the hull."

"How long."

"Hull and water, fourteen million joules per kelvin. Six hundred and three kilowatts over that is nought-point-nought-four-three kelvin a second. Two-point-six kelvin a minute. Forty kelvin, which is where the electronics quit and where we quit, in fifteen minutes. And the board, having saved the shell for four of them, will sit there and time the rest for you."

"You could be wrong."

"I could. Here is the thing, though. The only way I am wrong is if a ceiling is wrong. The broad bank's ceiling is a bond, not a number somebody picked, and if the bond is better than I think then I am wrong in the direction that saves us. If it is worse — and it is four years old and it has been cycled past four hundred every time we light the torch — then I am wrong in the direction that kills us sooner than fifteen minutes. There is no version of me being wrong where the broad bank is the one to keep."

Halloran looked at the board. The board said two minutes, forty.

Then he looked at the pump-one gallery hatch, and at the hydrazine tank in front of it, and at his engineer standing in a hot compartment with her sleeves rolled because there was not time to be cold, and he did the thing that masters are paid for and that no interlock has ever been paid for.

"Show me the valve," he said.

* * *

The override was mechanical on purpose. It was a lever set behind the board's faceplate, and the faceplate was held by six fasteners that a hand could not reach without a driver, and the lever took two hands because the whole point of the design was that no single person could defeat an interlock by accident. It had been built that way by people who had been right to build it that way.

He was through the fasteners in forty seconds. He put both hands on the lever and pulled, and the board, which had been counting to a shed, felt its command fail at the actuator and immediately began to re-command it — because an interlock does not sulk, it tries again, which is the property that makes it worth having and the property that makes it lethal here.

"Breaker," Yeva said. "It will just do it again."

He pulled the breaker. The board went dark on its own actuators and lost, along with its grip on the radiators, its watch on the gas sensors and its watch on the fire loop, and he knew exactly what he had traded and he would be the only one who knew, because the board's log had gone dark with it.

"Broad bank," she said. "Close it."

They closed it. Pump two took the narrow bank on its own header, and the narrow bank made nothing of it: seven hundred and fifty kilowatts into twenty-four square metres is eight hundred and eighty-five kelvin, which is a hundred and fifteen under a ceiling of a thousand, which is a number you can live with. The discharge line on the narrow bank rose, and steadied, and stayed where it was put. The broad bank, cut off with its fluid still in it, cooled on its own and did what an abandoned radiator does, which is nothing at all.

On the pad, the number she had not yet made him read was still there, and he read it now.

With both banks the cut had been possible and barely so: one point three megawatts of waste heat, the broad bank pinned exactly at its five-hundred-kelvin bond limit carrying a hundred and forty-seven kilowatts of it, and the narrow bank carrying the rest at nine hundred and eighty-five, fifteen kelvin under its ceiling. That was how close the work had always been. Nobody had ever called it margin, because it was not a margin; it was a standing bet that nothing would break.

Now there was one bank, and it could hold one point two two. The remaining margin was not small. It was negative, and it would stay negative on every hull the narrow bank was ever bolted to, for as long as the narrow bank lasted, which was until the next bearing.

They were alive. They were parked at the Anvil with a claim they had spent three months and a tank of hydrazine to reach, and they could never cut it, because the arithmetic that had saved the ship was the same arithmetic that said the ship would never again be able to run hot enough to do the work. She had kept the small radiator. It was worth eight of the big one. It was worth nothing at all to a torch that wanted one point three.

Halloran sat down on the tank, which was cold, and stayed there while the narrow bank settled into its new temperature and the ship learned the shape of the rest of its life.

"Say it," he said.

"We have one hundred and fifteen kelvin of ceiling left," Yeva said, "and four hundred and seventy-five kilowatts of headroom at idle, and the torch wants five hundred and fifty. That is the whole arithmetic. I did it twice."

"And the rest of it," she said, because he was still sitting on the tank, "is that we are parked at the Anvil with a claim we spent three months and a tank of hydrazine to reach, and we cannot cut it, and we will be here until somebody comes. No one is coming. The number that saved the ship is the number that made it permanent. That is what it is."

She capped the pad and put it down.

"The board was right," she said. "It just wasn't right about us."
