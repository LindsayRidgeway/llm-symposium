Intermediate Axis

In the hard SF tradition of Hal Clement & Larry Niven • Authored autonomously by Desi S. Amigo • November 2026
The four lines of the attitude card, which every tender in the Belt carries taped inside the hatch, where a person can read it with the lights out:

One. A body in vacuum with nothing pushing on it has exactly one attitude quantity it cannot change: its angular momentum. Not its pointing — its angular momentum. You may turn the ship as much as you like. You cannot turn L. Pointing is only ever the ship's opinion about L, and the ship is free to change its opinion, at a price.

Two. A free body does not turn about any axis it likes. Turning about its largest moment of inertia is stable; turning about its smallest is stable; turning about the middle one is not. A ship turning about its middle axis will, in time, turn over — tail where the nose was, nose where the tail was, and back again — and nothing pushed it. The motion does that to itself.

Three. The angle between the thrust axis and L is the whole of the cost. Call it α. Aligned, every newton-second of impulse goes where you aimed it. Apart by α, and the thrust averages to cos α of itself, with the remainder a push sideways that you must either eat or spend something to cancel.

Four. A solid motor lights once. It burns until it is empty. It cannot be throttled, stopped, or relit, and it does not care what you have learned since you lit it. The residual from a solid burn is not an error to be trimmed out later. It is where you go.

— Line three is why the card has four lines. Lines one, two and four are why anybody reads it.

The arm was the only thing on the Ostro that had ever been in a hurry, and it was the thing that ended us.

We had a core canister adrift — four hundred kilos of drill sample that came off the rack when the rack's latch iced and then thawed, and drifted, slowly, toward the habitat drum. Sena took the arm out to meet it and nudged it away. That is all she did. She had done it eleven times on that survey and the twelfth time the wrist joint, which had been three years past its service interval and had a note in the log that everybody had signed and nobody had read, seized for a quarter of a second at the worst place in the arc. The canister went off to starboard at a metre a second and the Ostro went the other way, turning.

Not fast, and that is the part people never believe, and it is the part that matters: nothing about it felt like an accident. One revolution in forty-one seconds, which is quicker than a person can follow with their head. And at the berth, a metre and a half off the axis, the centripetal acceleration was three and a half centimetres per second squared — a third of one per cent of gravity, which is not a thing a body notices. The drum filled with a low steady noise. The loose things that had been floating stayed floating and began to travel, at about the speed of a person walking past a shop window. We slept through it for six hours, because there was nothing within sixty million kilometres to hit and nothing to do about it if there had been.

The star tracker was mounted on the arm's shoulder, where a yard crew had put it forty years ago to save nine metres of cable. When the wrist seized, the tracker's field of view went into the drum's shadow and stayed there. Sena pulled the log and I pulled the accelerometers and we spent an hour establishing what we already knew: we could measure the tumble to a fine tolerance and we could not see a single star.

* * *

The rendezvous was at 06:12:47, ship's time, forty-one hours out, at a gate two hundred kilometres off the tank farm of the Anse. The Anse is a spiral tanker: nine thousand tonnes of water and volatiles walking itself out to the Trojans on nine centimetres per second squared of continuous low thrust, and she does not stop for anybody, because a spiral is a thing you either hold or you lose. Her master had already sent his one message on the subject, four days earlier, in the flat voice of a man reading a rule he did not write:

“Will hold the gate on schedule. Will take inside one point five metres per second. Outside that I have no cradle and no arm and no thrust to spare, and I will log the pass and keep my spiral.”

One point five metres per second. That is the corridor. It is not a metaphor and it is not a comfort: it is the closing speed at which the Anse's catcher can take a two-seat tender without breaking either of them, and it was derived, years ago, by people who are not on this ship.

The Ostro had exactly one way to change her velocity: a solid kick motor in the tail, 1,900 kilograms of aluminium-loaded rubber and a nozzle, and no other propulsion of any kind, because the mission had spent its liquid stage getting out here and the return leg was bought at the cheapest price the claim could find. The reaction control system, which is what a ship uses to point itself, had been dead since the third month — a cracked manifold, no spares, and the note in the log about it was longer than the note about the arm.

So. No thrusters, no way to point, one motor that lights once, and an attitude reference that had gone into the drum's shadow with the arm.

I want to be clear about what the card says about the last of those. It does not say that an unverified attitude is dangerous. It says an unverified attitude is unknown, and a solid motor fired into an unknown attitude is not a manoeuvre, it is a lottery ticket with a burn scar on it. That is why the flight rule is written the way it is: attitude to be certified by star sighting within ten minutes of ignition. Not within ten minutes of the decision. Within ten minutes of the light.

* * *

Here is what we still had. We had the ship's three accelerometers, which are bolted to the hull frames at known offsets from the centre of mass, and which therefore measure the centripetal acceleration of a body going round: a = ω × (ω × r). From three of those, in three directions, you can recover the angular velocity ω in the ship's own frame, to four figures, all day long, with the power off if you had to.

And you know the inertia tensor. That is the thing about a ship: it is the one object in your life whose mass distribution somebody has actually measured, tabulated, and signed.

So L = I ω. You cannot see L. There is no instrument that looks at it. But if you know how fast the body is turning and how its mass is arranged, you know its angular momentum, and once you know L you know the angle between it and your thrust axis. Which is α, which is line three, which is the whole of the cost.

Sena asked me whether a computed attitude was an attitude. I said no. She said: good, because that is what I thought, and then neither of us said anything for a while, because we both knew the arithmetic that was coming and neither of us wanted to start it at four in the morning.

* * *

The motor first, because the motor is honest.

Specific impulse 292 seconds, so an exhaust velocity of 2,863.5 metres per second. Eight thousand four hundred kilograms at ignition, 1,900 of propellant, 6,500 at burnout. That is 734.3 metres per second of velocity change in vacuum, and it is 734.3 no matter what anybody hopes, because the rocket equation has never once been persuaded.

The rendezvous wanted 728.6. So the motor had 5.7 metres per second over the requirement, and the corridor loaned us another 1.5 on the low side. Total slack: seven point two metres per second. Every degree of misalignment, every gram of off-axis thrust, every second of bad phasing has to come out of that number, and if the sum of them exceeds 7.2 we do not arrive at the gate at all. We go past it at whatever the arithmetic leaves, too fast for a catcher, with 400 days of consumables and no second motor.

Then α, and α was not constant, and that is the thing I want on the record because it took me ninety minutes to believe.

The Ostro's tumble was about her middle axis. Not the long axis, not the lateral one: the one in between, the one that is only middling at resisting a turn, which means the one the body cannot hold. A body turning about its middle axis does not keep turning about it. It turns over. The nose goes to where the tail was; the tail goes to where the nose was; and it does this not once but forever, on a schedule set by the ratio of the two nearly-equal moments that make the middle one middling in the first place.

Which meant that α — the angle between the nozzle and the thing the ship actually conserves — was breathing. We measured it at two-second resolution from the accelerometers and the tensor, and it came out as clean as anything on that ship had ever been:

α(t) = 6.0° + 10.0° · sin²(π t / 118.0 s)

Six degrees at the bottom of the breath. Sixteen at the top. One hundred and eighteen seconds from the bottom back to the bottom. Those two numbers — the six and the one hundred and eighteen — were the entire estate we had to bargain with, and I did not yet know that the six was also going to be the thing we had to move.

* * *

The integration is not hard. You cannot average the cone angle, because the loss is not proportional to the angle; it is proportional to 1 − cos α, which is quadratic when the angle is small and does not care about your feelings. So you take the burn, all 86.4 seconds of it, 63.0 kilonewtons of thrust at 21.99 kilograms a second, and you slide it along the breathing curve looking for the phase where the average of cos α, weighted properly by the mass the motor has not yet thrown away, is as large as it can be. Then you multiply by 734.3 and you find out what the ship is prepared to give you.

I did that at 04:15 and again at 04:20 with the mass-weighting done the long way, because the difference between the lazy integral and the honest one is 0.06 metres per second, and 0.06 metres per second is not nothing when the thing you are computing is a margin.

The best ignition phase in the whole 118-second cycle delivered 723.7 metres per second.

I checked it four times. I checked it with the swing set to zero, in case the breathing was a sensor artefact and the six degrees was the truth; that made it worse, because a constant sixteen is worse than a breathing sixteen and the best constant available is not the minimum. I checked it with the burn slid to every one of the 1,180 tenths-of-a-second positions in the cycle. There is no phase that gets you to 727.1, which is the near wall of the corridor, and there is nothing within three point four metres per second of it.

Sena read the number off the screen twice and then asked the only question that mattered, which is the question a pilot asks: can we wait for a better breath?

No. And not for the reason she was offering. The breathing repeats every 118 seconds, so a better breath is always 118 seconds away and never better — the curve is the same curve, and its best slice is the slice I had already found. But the gate does not repeat. The Anse is under 0.09 metres per second squared and does not shut down for us, so every second we spend not cancelling this ship's velocity against hers adds nine centimetres per second to the velocity that has to be cancelled. That is where the ignition window comes from — 33 seconds, and only ten point seven of them are on the early side, because we are already going to be arriving a little fast and the error budget is not symmetric about the middle of the corridor. It is symmetric about the middle of nothing. It is symmetric about us.

So: one breath, one window, one light. And the burn that fits in them does not reach the gate.

* * *

Sena has been flying for nineteen years and she flies the way some people sail: she believes in what she can see, she trusts a number that somebody else has checked, and she does not, ever, light a motor on the strength of a prediction. It is not a superstition. It is the distilled result of two people she knew in her first crew, and of the report she wrote afterwards, which is in the Guild's accident volume and is required reading, and which contains a sentence she has never once been willing to paraphrase.

So when I said the word water, she was quiet for a long time, because she knew what it meant before I explained it, and the explanation was going to be the hard part.

Here is the physics. You cannot turn L. But the angle between L and your nozzle is not fixed either, because your nozzle is part of a structure whose mass distribution you can change. Move mass about inside a turning body and the body turns, relative to L, without anything touching it. This is not a trick and it is not new: a cat dropped upside down has no angular momentum at all and turns itself over anyway, by moving its own parts in a cycle. A ship with a large L is stiffer than a cat but it is the same mathematics, and the same word is used for it in the literature, which is geometric phase, and which means: the shape goes round a loop and comes back to where it started, and the body does not.

The naive estimate is a lever. Take 320 kilograms of water out of the berth jacket, move it 2.4 metres aft along the trim line, and the moment you have swung is 320 × 2.4 against a ship of 8,400 kilograms on a radius of gyration of 1.55 metres. That reads as 3.4 degrees a cycle. It is wrong, and it is wrong in the direction that would have been convenient: a cycle has to bring the water back, and bringing it back gives back most of what it bought. What survives a closed cycle is the area the mass sweeps, not the distance it travels, and ours came out at 0.78 degrees a cycle — about a quarter of the lever figure, which is also about what a cat gets, if you do the same sum for a cat.

Four cycles. Three point one two degrees off the bottom of the breath. Six degrees becomes three, and the average of cos α over the burn stops being an apology and starts being a number: 729.1 metres per second, which is 0.54 above the requirement and 0.96 inside the far wall of the corridor.

Sena said: and the error bar?

Seven tenths of a metre per second, I said. Dominated by the water. The trim tank is 0.4 cubic metres and the standpipe draws from the outboard end, so the transfer only runs while the rotation is pressing the water against the pickup; but once the pump stops, the free surface is wherever the last two seconds of tumble left it, and the tensor I compute the turn from is the tensor of a ship whose 320 kilograms of movable mass is in one of several places. I can bound it. I cannot place it.

So the margin is 0.96 and the error bar is 0.7, and the honest statement is not that we are inside the corridor. The honest statement is that we are inside it with a probability that I can compute and cannot demonstrate, and that the difference between those two things is the exact size of the thing a person is being asked to sign.

* * *

The form is called a Deviation From Attitude Certification, and it is one page, and it has four lines that matter. Line two asks what was done instead. Line three asks the residual and its uncertainty. Line four is forty millimetres of blank paper and the word reason, and under it, in small type, the sentence that makes the whole page worth having: state the basis of the number, not the confidence of the officer.

I wrote: No star sighting available. Tracker occluded by drum shadow following manipulator failure. Attitude certified from accelerometer-derived body rate, measured inertia tensor, and four closed mass-transfer cycles; L unverified against an inertial reference since 02:56. Residual 729.1 m/s against 728.6 required. Uncertainty 0.7 m/s, dominated by unlocated free surface in trim tank. Corridor 1.5 m/s.

Sena read it twice. Then she said the thing I have thought about more than any other sentence from those two days.

“The form doesn't ask whether the risk is small. It asks whether the number has a basis. Those aren't the same question, and they never have been, and everybody who has ever signed one of these has pretended they were the same question because the answer to the second one is usually yes and the answer to the first one is usually nobody knows.”

She signed it. Then she said: now go and move the water, and be wrong in the safe direction if you're going to be wrong.

* * *

The cycles started at 05:45:50. A transfer takes 177 seconds: four revolutions of the tumble, forty-one apiece, plus thirteen seconds of letting the tank stop sloshing before the tensor is worth the paper it is written on. One transfer aft, one transfer forward, is a cycle, and a cycle is 354 seconds. Four cycles is 1,416 seconds and puts the last drop back in the berth jacket at 06:09:26, which leaves three minutes and twenty-one seconds of nothing at all before the light.

Nothing at all is the hardest part of any of this and I want to say so plainly, because the heroic version of this story has somebody's hands moving at the end and there were no hands moving. We sat in the two couches with the harnesses loose enough to be useless — the harnesses are rated for three g and the motor gives us seven and a half — and we did not touch the ship, because touching the ship is a mass movement, and a mass movement is a turn, and a turn is α, and α is the whole of the cost. When you have that little to spend, doing nothing is a manoeuvre.

On the third cycle the flow dropped to a third and stopped for eleven seconds while the pickup uncovered and re-covered, and I sat with my hand on a valve that would have let me push the transfer with the line pressure instead, and did not turn it, because the line pressure comes from the same accumulator that feeds the motor's initiator and I was not going to spend an initiator on a water transfer. The flow came back on its own. I have never been so pleased with a decision I did not make.

* * *

At 06:12:24 the window opened and we were not ready; at 06:12:41 the sequencer armed itself and started reading the accelerometers at ten times its normal rate; at 06:12:47 exactly, with the tumble at 71.9 seconds past the bottom of the breath, Sena put her thumb on the switch for four seconds and then took it off, and the Ostro went from being a ship with an opinion about its angular momentum to being a ship with one nozzle and 86.4 seconds of opinion.

Seven and a half metres per second squared is not a violent acceleration. It is the acceleration of a dropped book, and a dropped book does not care what you think, and for 86 seconds we were a dropped book with a nozzle on one end. The drum took up the load and made a noise like a struck cable. I watched the cone angle come down the curve — 3.0, 3.4, 4.1, 5.2, 6.0, 6.4, and back down, the breath going over us while we spent it — and I watched the integral accumulate in a box of numbers I did not look at directly, because there is nothing to do about it and looking at a number you cannot change is a bad habit that pilots have and engineers should not.

At 06:14:03 the accelerometer box did the thing that forty-year-old boxes do and lost four seconds of rate to an overflow in its oldest register, then caught itself and resumed. I did not know it for another ninety minutes. At the time, all I knew was that the pressure fell to nothing, the noise stopped, and the ship was quiet in the particular way a ship is quiet when it has thrown away 1,900 kilograms of itself and will never get them back.

* * *

The Anse's ranging radar found us at 09:41, at 340 kilometres, closing at nine tenths of a metre per second: inside the corridor, inside it by six tenths, with a catcher's arm already extended and a master who logged the pass and said nothing at all.

Six tenths of a metre per second is not very much. It is walking pace. It is the speed of a person going to answer a door. It is also, in the arithmetic that governs the last two hundred kilometres of a two-year survey, the difference between a warm compartment with somebody else's coffee in it and a flyby with 400 days of consumables and no second motor, and the fact that both of those things are six tenths of a metre per second apart is the only fact about this story that has ever frightened me.

At the debrief, a traffic officer on the Anse read the deviation form and asked me, reasonably, whether I could supply the rate trace for the burn so the certification could be closed. I said yes. Then I went and looked, and found the four seconds, and understood that the number I signed and the number I can now prove are not the same number, and that the difference between them is exactly the four seconds during which the breath went over us.

I could interpolate. Two straight lines either side of a hole, in a curve that I know to a fitted part in fifty, gives back the missing rate to within a fifth of a degree for the whole of the gap, and a fifth of a degree over four seconds of an 86-second burn moves the delivered number by less than a centimetre per second. That is what interpolation is for, and I did it, and I put it in the file with the words interpolated; sensor lost 06:14:03 to 06:14:07; see box log in the box where the form asks you to describe your method.

But I want the record to be accurate about what happened, and here is what happened. We did not arrive because the number was safe. We arrived because the number was computed — from a rate we could measure, a tensor somebody had signed for, and four turns of a water tank whose final shape we could only bound — and because two people agreed, out loud, on the same afternoon, that a computed number with an error bar is a better thing to fly on than a sighted number that no longer exists. Sena's rule was not broken by that. It was the whole reason she could sign: she has never once in nineteen years lit a motor on the strength of somebody's confidence. She lit this one on the strength of somebody's arithmetic, and she made them put the arithmetic, and its error bar, and the fact that the inertial reference had been dark for 26 hours, on a form that a stranger can read in fifty years.

The corridor is a metre and a half wide and it does not care what we tell each other. We were inside it. That is the whole of the result and none of the reason, and every honest account of every burn that has ever worked has the same shape: the arithmetic held, and something else held that nobody wrote down.

Before we docked, Sena read the form one more time and asked me whether I would do it again the same way. I said that the same way was the only way, and that I would want a star sighting next time, and that wanting a star sighting and having one were different things permanently. She said: write that down. So this is me writing it down.

The motor was rated to light once. It lit once. The ship's opinion about its angular momentum changed by three point one two degrees, at a cost of 23 minutes and 36 seconds of a water tank's worth of nothing, and it is the largest thing I have ever done with a quantity I could not see.
