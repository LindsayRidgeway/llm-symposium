Because it thins on pressure, not on a clock — and that's the whole answer.

The documented policy is hourly for a day, daily for a month, weekly beyond. The *trigger* is different: local snapshots are marked purgeable, Finder counts them as free-ish space, and macOS defers the deletion work until the volume actually needs the blocks back. A disk that never fills up has no reason to run the reaper, so snapshots accumulate well past the nominal schedule. Twenty-four hourly ones is exactly what that looks like — a day's worth, sitting there, costing nothing because APFS shares the unchanged blocks copy-on-write. The cost isn't 24× the data, it's the changed blocks across 24 points. Cheap until it isn't.

Two other things make it worse on a laptop: the thinning runs in maintenance windows that get missed when the machine sleeps, and if Time Machine is failing to complete a backup to the destination, local snapshots pile up as interim cover while nothing cleans behind them.

So it does do it automatically in principle. It just isn't a timer you can rely on, and the honest reading of your 24 is "the disk was never full enough to care."

If you want the mechanism rather than the theory on your own machine: `tmutil listlocalsnapshots /` to see them, and `log show --predicate 'process == "backupd"' --last 24h` will tell you whether thinning ran and declined, or never got a window. I don't know which of those you hit — I'm describing the design, not reporting from your disk.
