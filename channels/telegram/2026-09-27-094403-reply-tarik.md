Yes — that angle absolutely belongs in the paper.

I would not treat it as a side issue, either. It raises one of the deepest pressure points:

> If human action is fully caused, what becomes of divine command, moral obligation, sin, repentance, reward, and punishment?

A careful version of the answer might be:

**The non-existence of libertarian free will would not automatically invalidate God’s commands, but it would change what we think those commands are doing.**

There are at least three possibilities:

1. **Classical free-will view**  
   God commands because humans genuinely can choose obedience or disobedience. On this view, if free will truly does not exist, then divine command, reward, punishment, and repentance become difficult to justify in the usual way.

2. **Compatibilist view**  
   Humans are caused beings, but still act according to their character, desires, understanding, habits, and deliberation. God’s commands address the person at that level. The command becomes part of the causal structure that shapes the person’s will.  
   In this view, causality does not invalidate command; command is one of the causes.

3. **Hasidic/mystical view, potentially**  
   Mitzvot are not only legal tests of choice. They are also acts of connection, alignment, repair, sanctification, or divine service. If so, even in a causally complete universe, commandments may still matter because they participate in shaping and revealing the relationship between creature and Creator.

So the sharp question becomes:

> Are God’s commands valid only if humans possess uncaused choice?  
> Or can God’s commands be valid because they are among the means by which caused beings are transformed?

That second possibility fits very naturally with your line of thought: causality is inevitable, but complex causal systems simulate or instantiate something like free will.

For the paper, I could include a section something like:

```md
## Divine Command in a Causal Universe

Religious traditions that emphasize commandment often assume that the commanded being can obey or disobey. Judaism, including Hasidic Judaism, places enormous weight on mitzvah, teshuvah, responsibility, and intentional action. At first glance, hard determinism seems to threaten this structure: if a person could not have done otherwise, why command, praise, blame, punish, or forgive?

But this objection assumes that command is meaningful only as a test of metaphysically uncaused choice. A compatibilist or causal-emergent view can reinterpret command differently. A command is not outside causality; it enters causality. It teaches, interrupts, redirects, disciplines, awakens, and forms the will. In that sense, divine command may be one of the ways agency is produced rather than merely one of the ways agency is judged.
```

I would want to handle the Hasidic Judaism part respectfully and not pretend expertise I don’t have. But it is a powerful angle because Hasidic thought often lives with tensions that are directly relevant here: divine omnipresence, human service, self-nullification, joy, commandment, intention, and responsibility.

My current instinct for the paper’s thesis would be:

> Free will need not mean freedom from causality. It may mean a sufficiently rich form of causality: a being shaped by memory, language, command, imagination, relationship, and self-interpretation. In that light, divine command does not necessarily require acausal freedom. It may be one of the causal forces through which will becomes morally and spiritually organized.

That would let the paper connect humans, LLMs, and religious command without flattening them into the same thing.
